# I. TURKEY (Erdogan, 2013-Present)

## 1. The Post-2016 Coup Attempt Purge

The failed coup attempt of July 15, 2016 provided the Erdogan government with a sweeping pretext for the largest purge of state institutions in modern Turkish history. A state of emergency was declared on July 21, 2016 and extended seven times before ending on July 19, 2018. During this period, the government issued 32 emergency decrees, 12 of which included mass dismissals from public service ([Stockholm Center for Freedom, 2026](https://stockholmcf.org/turkeys-post-coup-crackdown-10-years-on-the-purge-of-public-servants/)).

### Scale of Dismissals

According to a ruling party lawmaker's statement in parliament in 2026, **162,239 public servants** were dismissed through emergency decrees and subsequent summary procedures. Other tallies place the figure at 134,258 based on official dismissal lists. By July 2026, Turkish authorities had initiated legal proceedings against **720,338 people** over alleged links to the Gulen movement and secured 127,102 convictions ([Stockholm Center for Freedom, "A Decade's Legacy"](https://stockholmcf.org/turkeys-post-coup-crackdown-10-years-on-a-decades-legacy-2/)).

### Agencies Most Affected

- **Judiciary:** More than **4,500 judges and prosecutors** — roughly a quarter of the entire judiciary — were dismissed by the Supreme Board of Judges and Prosecutors (HSYK). On July 16, 2016, one day after the coup was foiled, 2,745 judges were detained in the first wave alone ([Wikipedia, Purges in Turkey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016%E2%80%93present_purges_in_Turkey)).

- **Military:** The Turkish Armed Forces expelled **26,206 members**. Former Chief of Staff Necdet Ozel estimated it would take 3-5 years to rebuild the military even after the smaller Ergenekon-era purges; rebuilding after the 2016 purge posed a far greater challenge ([Middle East Institute](https://mei.edu/publication/post-coup-purge-adds-turkeys-instability/)).

- **Education:** 15,000 people were fired from the education ministry, 21,000 private school teachers had licenses revoked, and approximately 2,000 institutions (secondary schools and universities) were closed ([Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/2016/07/20/turkey-s-post-coup-purge-widens-to-schools-and-universities)).

- **Police:** 2,687 police officers were dismissed in a single decree round ([NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/storyline/turkey-military-coup/turkey-post-coup-purge-15-000-more-government-employees-fired-n687181)).

- **Civil Service Broadly:** Dismissals included tax inspectors, midwives, and other state employees, and 375 institutions were shut down entirely ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/amp/news/2017/7/14/turkey-dismisses-thousands-of-police-and-civil-servants)).

### "Civil Death"

The purge created what rights groups describe as a decade of "civil death" — dismissed individuals had passports cancelled, assets frozen, and were effectively barred from future public employment, creating a class of civic non-persons ([Turkish Minute](https://turkishminute.com/2026/06/16/turkeys-post-coup-purge-created-a-decade-of-civil-death-rights-group-says/)).

## 2. Statistical Reliability: TurkStat (TUIK) vs. ENAG

### Firing of TurkStat Leadership

In **January 2022**, President Erdogan dismissed TurkStat head Sait Erdal Dincer after the institute reported that annual inflation had reached a 19-year high of 36.1%. He was replaced by Erhan Cetinkaya, a banking regulation official. Since April 2019, the head of TurkStat had been replaced multiple times, with leadership changes occurring when reported figures exceeded government expectations ([Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2022-01-29/turkey-replaces-turkstat-chief-as-reliability-debate-spills-over); [Turkish Minute](https://www.turkishminute.com/2022/03/17/ogan-sacks-two-vice-chairmen-at-turkstat/)).

The **International Statistical Institute (ISI) and International Association for Official Statistics (IAOS)** issued a joint statement expressing "deep concerns" over the dismissal, saying it "undermined significantly" TurkStat's credibility.

### The ENAG Divergence

In September 2020, a group of Istanbul-based academics founded the **Inflation Research Group (ENAG)** to publish an independent, transparent price index. The gap has been dramatic:

| Period | TurkStat (TUIK) | ENAG | Ratio |
|--------|-----------------|------|-------|
| March 2022 | 61% | 143% | 2.3x |
| February 2026 | 31.53% | 54.14% | 1.7x |

([Bianet](https://bianet.org/haber/turkstat-and-enag-inflation-figures-show-twofold-discrepancy-308038); [P.A. Turkey](https://www.paturkey.com/news/2026/inflation-divide-tuik-and-enag-reveal-massive-gap-in-february-data-28374/))

A 2025 lawsuit alleged that Turkey's inflation data is "deliberately underreported," with TurkStat refusing to provide details on inflation basket contents, individual item prices, or their respective weights — and falsely claiming that prices were available on its website ([Nordic Monitor](https://nordicmonitor.com/2025/03/turkeys-inflation-data-is-deliberately-underreported-lawsuit-alleges-casting-doubts-on-reliability-of-official-statistics/)).

In 2025, TurkStat sued an opposition MP for claiming inflation manipulation ([Turkish Minute](https://www.turkishminute.com/2025/08/05/turkeys-statistics-agency-sues-opposition-mp-over-inflation-manipulation-claim/)).

## 3. Judiciary: Progressive Capture

### Pre-Referendum Erosion (2014-2017)

Due process protections began eroding around 2014, when the government reassigned thousands of police officers, judges, and prosecutors in response to corruption investigations that implicated Erdogan's inner circle ([Freedom House, Turkey 2026](https://freedomhouse.org/country/turkey/freedom-world/2026)).

### The 2017 Constitutional Referendum

On April 16, 2017, Turkey narrowly approved (51.4%) constitutional amendments shifting governance from a parliamentary to a presidential system. The **Venice Commission** warned that the president's dual role as head of state and dominant force in parliamentary party politics would allow him to control judicial appointments from both the presidency and parliament, placing "the independence of the judiciary in serious jeopardy" ([Human Rights Watch](https://www.hrw.org/news/2017/04/04/questions-and-answers-turkeys-constitutional-referendum)).

Key changes included:
- The president gained direct control over appointments to the renamed **Council of Judges and Prosecutors (HSK)**
- The judiciary was effectively subordinated to the president
- The president gained power to appoint senior judges, including to the Constitutional Court
- Legislative oversight was significantly weakened

([NYU Globalex](https://www.nyulawglobal.org/globalex/2017_turkey_constitution_amendments.html); [OSW Commentary](https://www.osw.waw.pl/en/publikacje/osw-commentary/2017-04-28/constitutional-referendum-another-step-towards-a-new-turkey))

Turkey now scores **0 out of 4** on Freedom House's "Right to a Fair Trial" indicator — the worst score globally ([Freedom House 2026](https://freedomhouse.org/country/turkey/freedom-world/2026)).

## 4. University Purges and the "Academics for Peace" Petition

### The Petition

On January 11, 2016, **1,128 academics** signed a petition titled "We will not be party to this crime," condemning the government's military operations in southeastern Turkey. Despite reprisals, signatures eventually grew to **2,212** ([Wikipedia, Academics for Peace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academics_for_Peace)).

### Consequences

- All 1,128 original signatories then in Turkey were placed under criminal investigation
- Signatories were charged with "propagandizing for a terrorist organization" (up to 7.5 years in prison)
- At least 100 Academics for Peace signatories were dismissed from their positions before the state of emergency was even declared
- After the July 2016 coup attempt, the purge expanded: a November 2016 decree ordered the dismissal of 242 academic personnel and 942 higher education administrative staff
- Across all emergency decrees, approximately **6,000 academics** were dismissed from universities

([Human Rights Watch](https://www.hrw.org/news/2017/12/05/turkey-academics-trial-signing-petition); [Scholars at Risk](https://www.scholarsatrisk.org/actions/academics-for-peace-turkey/); [MERIP](https://www.merip.org/2018/12/turkeys-purge-of-critical-academia/))

As of 2025, some academics are only now being reinstated — nine years after their emergency-decree dismissals ([Stockholm Center for Freedom](https://stockholmcf.org/turkish-academic-who-signed-2016-peace-petition-reinstated-9-years-after-emergency-decree-dismissal/)).

## 5. Speed, Irreversibility, and Brain Drain

### What Has Proved Impossible to Rebuild

The unprecedented scale of institutional purges has created lasting damage:
- Replacing thousands of military officers and police proved far more challenging than after previous, smaller purges
- The judiciary lost a quarter of its personnel; replacements were selected for loyalty rather than competence
- University closures and academic dismissals disrupted research programs and curricula that took decades to build

### Brain Drain Statistics

Total emigration surged from **177,960 in 2016 to 714,579 in 2023** — a fourfold increase. Among higher education graduates specifically:
- The brain drain rate rose from **1.6% in 2015 to 2.0% in 2023-2024**
- Highest-drain fields: molecular biology and genetics (17.9%), bioengineering (10.2%), management engineering (9.8%), electronics engineering (9.1%), computer engineering (8.4%)
- Information and communication technology graduates emigrate at 6.7%, engineering graduates at 4.4%
- 21% of emigrating graduates preferred the United States as a destination

The brain circulation rate (returnees) was 41.9% of those who had gone abroad between 2010 and 2022 — meaning nearly 60% did not return ([Duvar English](https://www.duvarenglish.com/brain-drain-of-turkish-higher-education-graduates-increases-to-2-pct-in-2023-news-64998); [Turkish Minute](https://www.turkishminute.com/2025/10/24/turkeys-brain-drain-led-by-engineering-and-it-graduates-official-data-show/)).

A 2026 study on physician brain drain found that push factors — political environment, working conditions, low pay — were dominant, with most physician emigrants expressing low return intentions ([Springer, Health Care Analysis 2026](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10728-026-00587-w)).

# II. INDIA (BJP/Modi, 2014-Present)

## 1. Statistical Agencies: GDP and Employment Data Reliability

### The 2015 GDP Methodology Controversy

In 2015, the Modi government introduced a new methodology, changing the base year from 2004-05 to 2011-12 and switching from GDP at factor cost to GDP at market prices. While the alignment with international standards was legitimate in principle, the revision incorporated corporate financial filings (from the Ministry of Corporate Affairs) as a primary data source while phasing out traditional sources like the Index of Industrial Production and Annual Survey of Industries. Critics raised concerns about the use of a single deflator for converting nominal to real GDP growth, diverging from the internationally recognized double deflation method ([Ideas for India](https://www.ideasforindia.in/topics/macroeconomics/indias-gdp-makeover-what-is-changing); [The Federal](https://thefederal.com/the-federal-special/how-not-to-revise-gdp-base-year-to-bring-back-credibility-of-data-192107)).

The 2018 back-series data release intensified controversy, with revised figures showing 8.6% growth that diverged significantly from other economic indicators. Numerous academic studies have identified problems with the data and methodologies, denting the credibility of India's National Accounts Statistics ([Business Standard](https://www.business-standard.com/amp/article/economy-policy/year-ender-2018-the-release-of-gdp-back-series-was-long-overdue-118122500605_1.html)).

### The Suppressed NSSO Employment Report (2019)

India's unemployment rate hit a **45-year high of 6.1%** during 2017-18, according to the NSSO's periodic labour force survey — higher than the previous peak reached in 1972-73. The labour force participation rate (LFPR) fell sharply from 55.9% in 2011-12 to 49.8% in 2017-18 ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2019/1/31/india-unemployment-rate-highest-in-45-years-report); [The Quint](https://www.thequint.com/news/india/unemployment-india-45-year-high-nsso-report)).

The controversy erupted when:
- The report's scheduled December 2018 release was delayed without explanation
- **Two members of the National Statistical Commission resigned**, including acting chairman P.C. Mohanan, alleging interference by state agencies
- The government dismissed the leaked report as "not final, not verified"
- Mohanan publicly contradicted this, confirming the report was "final"
- The timing — immediately before the 2019 general elections — fueled allegations of politically motivated suppression

([Newslaundry](https://www.newslaundry.com/2019/02/01/how-did-the-media-cover-business-standards-report-on-nsso-unemployment-numbers); [Deccan Herald](https://www.deccanherald.com/amp/story/india%2Fmodi-govts-suppresion-715863.html))

## 2. Election Commission Independence

### The 2023 Supreme Court Ruling

In March 2023, a five-judge Constitution Bench headed by Justice K.M. Joseph ruled that appointments to the Chief Election Commissioner and Election Commissioners must be made by a three-member committee comprising: (1) the Prime Minister, (2) the Leader of the Opposition in the Lok Sabha, and (3) the **Chief Justice of India**. The Court noted that the Constituent Assembly intended the Election Commission's independence to be regulated by law, not decided by the Executive alone ([JURIST](https://www.jurist.org/news/2023/03/india-dispatch-supreme-court-ruling-on-election-commission-appointments-limits-executive-branch-discretion/)).

### The Government's Legislative Override

Parliament enacted the **Chief Election Commissioner and Other Election Commissioners (Appointment, Conditions of Service and Terms of Office) Act, 2023**, which deviated from the Court's directive by **replacing the Chief Justice of India with a Union Cabinet Minister** nominated by the Prime Minister. The revised selection committee — Prime Minister, a Cabinet Minister, and the Leader of the Opposition — gives the ruling government a built-in 2-to-1 majority ([PRS Legislative Research](https://prsindia.org/billtrack/prs-products/prs-legislative-brief-4256)).

The Attorney General conceded during subsequent hearings that the Prime Minister's nominee would "probably" not take a different position in cases of disagreement. Justice Datta responded that this was enough to undermine public perception of independence, asserting that the Election Commission "must not only be independent, but appear to be independent" ([Bar and Bench](https://www.barandbench.com/amp/story/news/why-put-up-a-show-of-independence-in-election-commission-appointments-supreme-court-asks-centre)).

A split Supreme Court verdict in 2026 sent the challenge to this law to a larger bench, leaving the matter unresolved ([Supreme Court Observer](https://www.scobserver.in/reports/sc-delivers-split-verdict-sends-eci-appointments-law-challenge-to-larger-bench/)).

## 3. Media Ownership and Press Freedom

### Ownership Concentration

A Reporters Without Borders study found that a majority of leading Indian news outlets are controlled by individuals with political ties. The print media market is highly concentrated, with just four publications capturing three out of four readers in Hindi ([360info](https://360info.org/the-corporate-takeover-of-indias-media/)).

### The Adani-NDTV Acquisition (2022)

India's richest man, **Gautam Adani** — widely perceived as closely aligned with the Modi government — acquired a **64.71% stake in NDTV**, one of the last major independent media outlets in India. The acquisition triggered the departure of many leading NDTV journalists. RSF called it a signal of "the end of pluralism in India's leading media" ([RSF](https://rsf.org/en/ndtv-takeover-signals-end-pluralism-india-s-leading-media); [Vice](https://www.vice.com/en/article/billionaire-adani-hostile-takeover-ndtv-india-press-freedom/); [Japan Times](https://www.japantimes.co.jp/news/2024/02/26/asia-pacific/politics/billionaire-press-barons-india/)).

## 4. Civil Service (IAS) Politicization

### Premature Transfers

According to Haryana's Civil Services Board annual report, IAS officers were transferred **120 times in one year** before completing the two-year minimum tenure mandated by the Supreme Court. In one June 2025 transfer order, 22 of 30 officers were moved from positions they had held for less than two years. The reason cited in every case was "administrative exigencies and public interest" ([The Print](https://theprint.in/india/governance/120-premature-transfers-in-a-year-how-haryana-shredded-supreme-courts-tenure-rules-for-ias-officers/2830931/)).

Political loyalty has been documented as influencing transfers, suspensions, and promotions, with upright officers reportedly isolated through harassment and punitive postings ([Indian Republic](https://www.indianrepublic.in/2026/05/how-political-transfers-undermine-indian-bureaucracy.html)).

### Lateral Entry

The lateral entry scheme, introduced in 2018 to bring private sector domain experts into Joint Secretary and Director positions, reached 63 appointments by July 2024. However, **45 positions were cancelled in August 2024** due to reservation (affirmative action) concerns. Critics raised alarms about the potential for ideologically motivated selections ([Vajirao & Ravi](https://vajiramandravi.com/current-affairs/lateral-entry/); [The Print](https://theprint.in/india/governance/modi-govt-is-shaking-the-foundation-of-indias-ias-led-civil-service-one-reform-at-a-time/291893/)).

## 5. Courts: Mixed Record of Independence

### Delayed Hearings on Politically Sensitive Cases

The Supreme Court has been criticized for slow action on politically charged cases:
- **Article 370 (Kashmir):** The Court upheld the 2019 abrogation only in December 2023, more than four years after the revocation
- **Electoral Bonds:** Filed in 2018, struck down only in February 2024
- **Citizenship Amendment Act (CAA):** Challenges filed in 2019 remained unresolved for years

### The Electoral Bonds Ruling (February 2024)

On February 15, 2024, the Supreme Court **unanimously struck down the Electoral Bonds Scheme**, holding that it violated voters' right to information under Article 19(1)(a). The five-judge bench directed the State Bank of India to disclose all bond purchasers and political party recipients. Given the Court's recent record of largely siding with the BJP government on politically fraught issues, this was considered momentous ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2024/2/15/indias-supreme-court-scraps-electoral-bonds-calls-it-unconstitutional); [Foreign Policy](https://foreignpolicy.com/2024/04/23/india-election-judicial-autonomy-electoral-bonds-modi/); [Global Voices](https://globalvoices.org/2024/04/16/democratizing-elections-the-supreme-court-of-indias-landmark-verdict-on-electoral-bonds/)).

This mixed record — delayed hearings punctuated by occasional independent rulings — illustrates partial, rather than complete, judicial capture.

## 6. Federalism as Resilience

### How Opposition States Have Pushed Back

India's federal structure has provided significant resistance to centralization:

- **Kerala, Tamil Nadu, and West Bengal** remain outside the BJP's "double engine" governance model and have presented federalism as a value under active threat ([The Wire](https://m.thewire.in/article/culture/why-kerala-tamil-nadu-and-west-bengal-remain-outside-the-bjps-double-engine-model))

- Opposition-ruled states have fought legal battles against the central government over revenue sharing, disaster relief funds, and governance prerogatives

- States have publicly condemned the **politicization of the office of Governor**, with BJP-appointed governors blocking state legislation and obstructing development initiatives in opposition-ruled states ([Tribune India](https://www.tribuneindia.com/news/comment/opposition-states-battle-growing-central-dominance/))

- In 2026, the DMK (Tamil Nadu) joined Kerala's Chief Minister in protesting central government overreach ([Deccan Herald](https://www.deccanherald.com/india/tamil-nadu/dmk-mps-to-join-kerala-cms-protest-against-union-government-2882581))

- India's 2026 state elections saw upsets in Tamil Nadu and West Bengal, reinforcing that the BJP's reach remains limited in significant parts of the country ([ISAS NUS](https://www.isas.nus.edu.sg/papers/indias-2026-state-elections-upsets-in-tamil-nadu-and-west-bengal/))

## 7. What Has Kept India from Following Hungary's Path?

Several structural factors have prevented India from following Hungary's trajectory toward full authoritarian consolidation:

1. **Federalism:** India's 28 states and 8 union territories create multiple veto points. Opposition-ruled states control their own police, education systems, and significant governance capacity, making national-level institutional capture insufficient for total control.

2. **Judicial Independence (Partial):** The Supreme Court retains more formal autonomy than Hungary's did, demonstrated by rulings like the 2024 electoral bonds decision. The collegium system for judicial appointments, while imperfect, has resisted full executive control.

3. **Size and Diversity:** India's linguistic, cultural, religious, and regional diversity makes any single-party-captures-all scenario structurally harder than in Hungary's relatively homogeneous polity of 10 million.

4. **Opposition States as Laboratories of Resistance:** States like Kerala, Tamil Nadu, and West Bengal have served as institutional counterweights, maintaining alternative governance models and challenging central policies through courts and public mobilization.

5. **Democratic Tradition:** India has a longer, if uneven, democratic tradition with multiple moments of recovery — including the reversal of Indira Gandhi's Emergency (1975-77) — providing historical precedent for democratic resilience.

([The Wire](https://m.thewire.in/article/world/what-india-can-learn-from-hungarys-democratic-comeback); [Verfassungsblog](https://verfassungsblog.de/reimagining-indian-federalism/))

# III. UNITED STATES (2025-Present)

## 1. Agencies Targeted First

### DOGE (Department of Government Efficiency)

Created by executive order and led by Elon Musk, DOGE operatives gained access to sensitive data systems across the federal government. The Wall Street Journal found that by February 2025, the units most affected by DOGE contract terminations were: **USAID, HHS, SSA, the Department of Education, and GSA** ([Time](https://time.com/7222251/doge-musk-federal-workers-government/); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/US_federal_agencies_targeted_by_DOGE)).

### USAID

USAID's independent operations ended on **July 1, 2025**, by which time **94% of its staff had been laid off**. The workforce was reduced from more than 10,000 to fewer than 300. Secretary of State Rubio announced that **83% of USAID programs** (over 5,200 contracts worth tens of billions) were being canceled, and remaining functions were absorbed into the State Department ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/sections/goats-and-soda/2025/07/01/g-s1-75222/usaid-trump-humanitarian-rubio-musk); [CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/amp/news/secretary-of-state-usaid-programs-canceled)).

### CFPB (Consumer Financial Protection Bureau)

On February 10, 2025, Acting Director Russell Vought announced the CFPB was closed. A federal judge blocked the closure, but by November 2025 the administration declared the agency's funding structure unlawful and signaled it would run out of money by early 2026 ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2025/3/28/judge-blocks-trump-effort-to-shutter-consumer-financial-protection-bureau); [EPI](https://www.epi.org/policywatch/trump-administration-closes-the-cfpb/)).

### Department of Education

On March 20, 2025, Trump issued an executive order to abolish the Department. By March, nearly **1,300 employees — almost half the workforce** — were terminated, with the Office for Civil Rights and student aid divisions hit hardest. The Supreme Court greenlighted further dismantling in a July 2025 ruling ([Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/graphics/2026-how-to-dismantle-education-department/); [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2025/11/18/nx-s1-5607221/education-department-trump); [NEA](https://www.nea.org/nea-today/all-news-articles/plan-abolish-education-department-one-year-later)).

### EPA, FBI/DOJ

Budget cuts to the EPA were implemented as part of the broader Project 2025 agenda. At the DOJ and FBI, leadership was replaced with appointees loyal to the president, and career attorneys departed in significant numbers ([Project 2025 tracking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Project_2025)).

## 2. Personnel Mechanisms

### Schedule F

On January 28, 2025, Trump issued an executive order stripping legal protections from political firings for thousands of federal employees. The Office of Personnel Management proposed reclassifying **50,000 federal employees** under a new Schedule F, ending civil service protections that prevent political firings ([EPI](https://www.epi.org/policywatch/eo-on-implementing-the-doge-workforce-optimization-initiative/)).

### Mass Firings

- **"Saint Valentine's Day Massacre" (February 14, 2025):** Acting OPM director signed directives instructing agencies to dismiss probationary employees (those with less than one year of service). Approximately **24,000 probationary workers** received termination notices in February-March 2025.
- Courts later found many terminations illegal, and **25,000 fired federal workers were reinstated** in March after courts ruled the probationary terminations unlawful ([Federal News Network](https://federalnewsnetwork.com/workforce/2025/03/25000-fired-feds-reinstated-after-courts-find-probationary-terminations-illegal/)).
- Total federal employee departures in 2025: approximately **317,000** — but about 92% were voluntary (retirements, deferred resignations, buyouts, normal attrition). Of the involuntary separations: ~6,900 probationary firings and ~17,000 RIFs that survived legal challenge ([Federal Pension Advisors](https://www.federalpensionadvisors.com/post/how-many-federal-employees-have-been-fired); [Federal Worker Rights](https://federalworkerrights.com/2025/12/18/doges-war-on-the-federal-workforce-by-the-numbers/); [TT News](https://www.ttnews.com/article/federal-workforce-down-317000)).

## 3. Independent Agencies Under Attack

### FTC Commissioners Fired

On **March 18, 2025**, the administration fired both Democratic FTC Commissioners — **Alvaro Bedoya and Rebecca Kelly Slaughter** — effective immediately. Both argued their firings were illegal and sued. The Supreme Court ultimately permitted Trump's firing of an FTC commissioner to remain in place in September 2025, marking a significant shift in the status of independent agencies ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2025/03/18/trump-fires-both-democratic-commissioners-at-ftc.html); [SCOTUSblog](https://www.scotusblog.com/2025/09/supreme-court-permits-trumps-firing-of-ftc-commissioner-to-remain-in-place/)).

### NLRB

Trump fired NLRB Member **Gwynne Wilcox** and Merit Systems Protection Board Member **Cathy Harris**. Two federal judges in Washington, D.C. ruled these firings violated federal law, but the firings remained in effect during appeals ([US News](https://www.usnews.com/news/us/articles/2025-03-27/two-fired-ftc-commissioners-sue-trump-administration)).

### Federal Reserve Independence

Trump repeatedly attacked Fed Chair **Jerome Powell** for not lowering interest rates, threatened to force him out, and the DOJ launched an investigation into Powell that observers deemed politically motivated. Trump attempted to remove Governor **Lisa Cook**; the Supreme Court intervened to keep her in place for the time being. Trump loyalist **Kevin Warsh** was being positioned to replace Powell as chair ([International Banker](https://internationalbanker.com/finance/trump-puts-federal-reserve-independence-under-increasing-threat/); [PBS](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/show/how-trumps-attempts-to-control-federal-reserve-board-threaten-its-long-held-independence); [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2025/04/18/nx-s1-5368036/is-trump-threatening-the-feds-independence-with-attacks-on-fed-chair-jerome-powell)).

## 4. Statistical Agencies Under Pressure

Federal statistical agencies face "increasingly overt politicization by the Trump Administration on top of chronic underinvestment" ([CBPP](https://www.cbpp.org/research/poverty-and-inequality/federal-data-are-disappearing-as-statistical-agencies-face-budget)).

### Census Bureau

- Director **Robert Santos resigned** in February 2025
- The Bureau has lost an estimated **15% of staff** since FY24
- A hiring freeze remains in place, with employees facing deadlines for early retirement and voluntary separation
- Staff departures have raised warnings about the Bureau's ability to produce reliable statistics, including for the decennial census ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/2025/04/17/nx-s1-5349435/us-census-bureau-data-decennial))

### Bureau of Labor Statistics

- BLS has lost an estimated **22% of staffing** since FY24
- The President's FY2026 budget proposed reducing BLS staffing from 2,175 to 1,999 FTEs (an 8% cut), with an 11.8% inflation-adjusted funding cut
- The administration has taken actions to "stop collecting or restrict access to federal statistical data," with some changes attributable to understaffing but others appearing politically motivated ([American Statistical Association](https://www.amstat.org/docs/default-source/amstat-documents/the-nations-data-at-risk-2025/bureau-of-labor-statistics.pdf); [Center for American Progress](https://www.americanprogress.org/article/the-next-bureau-of-labor-statistics-commissioner-must-restore-trust-in-the-agency-while-managing-fiscal-constraints/))

## 5. Inspector General Mass Firing

On **January 24, 2025**, President Trump fired at least **17 inspectors general** at federal government cabinet departments and agencies in a late-night action. The firings violated the statutory requirement to provide Congress with **30 days' notice and substantive rationale** (as amended in 2022) for removing an IG ([Lawfare](https://www.lawfaremedia.org/article/trump-fired-17-inspectors-general-was-it-legal); [ABC News](https://abcnews.com/Politics/trump-fires-17-independent-watchdogs-multiple-agencies-late/story?id=118097873); [EPI](https://www.epi.org/policywatch/trump-fires-17-inspectors-general/)).

On September 24, 2025, federal judge **Ana C. Reyes** ruled that the firings were **unlawful** but declined to reinstate the IGs, reasoning that Trump could simply re-fire them after providing the required 30-day notice. The administration also illegally defunded the council of IGs and closed whistleblower reporting portals ([Federal News Network](https://federalnewsnetwork.com/agency-oversight/2025/09/trump-unlawfully-fired-17-agency-igs-judge-finds-but-wont-reinstate-them/); [American Oversight](https://americanoversight.org/investigation/trumps-illegal-firing-of-inspectors-general/)).

## 6. Courts as Resistance

### Scale of Judicial Pushback

As of **May 14, 2025**, federal courts had issued **39 injunctions** against the second Trump administration, blocking actions including mass layoffs, federal funding freezes, and deportation actions. Key rulings:

- **DOGE/Treasury access:** Judge Paul Engelmayer issued an emergency preliminary injunction blocking DOGE from accessing the Treasury payment system. Eighteen states joined the action ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/2025/02/08/g-s1-47350/states-sue-to-stop-doge-accessing-personal-data)).

- **Privacy protections:** A federal judge blocked DOGE from accessing personal data at the Education Department, Treasury, and OPM ([PBS](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/politics/judge-temporarily-blocks-doge-from-accessing-sensitive-information-at-agencies)).

- **Federal workforce reduction:** Judge Susan Illston issued a preliminary injunction on May 22, 2025 blocking implementation of executive orders to reorganize agencies. The Ninth Circuit upheld the injunction on May 30. However, **the Supreme Court lifted the lower court decision on July 8**, siding with the administration ([Altshuler Berzon](https://altshulerberzon.com/federal-district-court-issues-preliminary-injunction-blocking-dismantling-of-government-agencies/)).

- **Probationary firings:** Courts found probationary terminations illegal and ordered reinstatement of ~25,000 workers.

### Compliance Problems

Courts have repeatedly noted the administration's pattern of non-compliance with court orders, slow-walking reinstatements, and using legal procedural delays to entrench changes before injunctions take effect ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/2025/02/15/nx-s1-5297925/courts-block-trumps-doge-actions-chaos-panic-not-proving-to-be-best-legal-strategy)).

### Supreme Court's Role

The Supreme Court has played a mixed role — blocking some lower court injunctions (lifting the workforce-reduction injunction in July 2025) while preserving others (keeping Fed Governor Lisa Cook in place). Its July 2025 ruling in **Trump v. CASA** substantially limited universal injunctions, reducing the ability of district courts to block nationwide policies ([SCOTUSblog](https://www.scotusblog.com/2025/09/supreme-court-permits-trumps-firing-of-ftc-commissioner-to-remain-in-place/); [Sidley Austin](https://www.sidley.com/en/insights/newsupdates/2025/07/supreme-court-substantially-limits-universal-injunctions)).

## 7. Speed Comparison: US 2025 vs. Hungary Year 1 (2010) and Poland Year 1 (2015)

The Carnegie Endowment's August 2025 report, **"U.S. Democratic Backsliding in Comparative Perspective"** (by McKenzie Carrier and Thomas Carothers), found:

- The U.S. aligns with patterns of **executive aggrandizement** seen in Hungary, India, and Turkey
- However, the U.S. is distinguished by **"the speed and breadth"** of these efforts
- The **"startling speed and multilayered nature of Trump's actions contrasts with the incremental, piece by piece nature of backsliding in most other troubled democracies"**
- In Poland, most backsliding changes occurred over 2015-2017; in Hungary, Orban's supermajority enabled comprehensive constitutional overhaul
- Poland maintained sufficient institutional resilience to allow **electoral reversal in 2023**; Hungary's backsliding proved deeper and harder to reverse

([Carnegie Endowment](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2025/08/us-democratic-backsliding-in-comparative-perspective))

### V-Dem's Assessment of Speed

V-Dem's 2026 Democracy Report found:
- Within **one year**, the USA's Liberal Democracy Index score declined by **24%**
- The USA's world rank dropped from **20th to 51st** out of 179 nations
- V-Dem downgraded the U.S. from a "liberal democracy" to an **"electoral democracy"**
- V-Dem's measures of legislative and judicial constraints on executive power fell to their **lowest levels in over 100 years**
- The USA tops the list for "the greatest deterioration in the executive oversight indicator in 2025"

V-Dem concluded: "Democracy in the USA is deteriorating at unprecedented speed" ([V-Dem Institute](https://www.v-dem.net/news/press-release-democratic-backsliding-reaches-western-democracies-with-us-decline-unprecedented/); [Gothenburg University](https://www.gu.se/en/news/democratic-backsliding-reaches-western-democracies-with-us-decline-unprecedented)).

## 8. What Has Proved Resilient So Far

### Courts

Federal courts have been the primary check, issuing dozens of injunctions. However, their effectiveness has been eroded by: (a) compliance problems, (b) the Supreme Court's willingness to lift lower court injunctions, and (c) the *Trump v. CASA* ruling limiting universal injunctions.

### State Attorneys General

Democratic attorneys general have filed **76 lawsuits** against the federal government since January 2025, **winning 42 of the 52 cases** that have seen rulings. Thousands of departed federal lawyers have moved to state AG offices, strengthening their capacity ([ABA](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/crsj/resources/human-rights/2026-march/litigation-resistance-protect-civil-rights-democracy/); [Stateline](https://stateline.org/2026/01/07/democratic-state-ags-will-lead-opposition-to-trump-in-new-year/)).

### Civil Society

More than **4,500 civil society organizations** sent an open letter to Congress in February 2025. Major foundations including Open Society Foundations and MacArthur Foundation increased funding for organizations facing targeting. CIVICUS noted that "civil society organisations are holding the line" ([CIVICUS](https://lens.civicus.org/interview/usa-civil-society-organisations-are-holding-the-line-they-are-not-hiding/)).

# IV. CROSS-COUNTRY COMPARISON

## Common Patterns

1. **Statistical agencies as early targets:** All three governments have undermined the independence and credibility of official statistics — Turkey by firing TurkStat heads who reported inconvenient numbers, India by suppressing the NSSO employment report and adopting contested GDP methodologies, and the US by cutting staff at BLS (-22%) and Census (-15%) while restricting data collection.

2. **Judiciary as battleground:** Turkey achieved near-complete judicial capture through the 2017 referendum. India has partially compromised judicial independence through delayed appointments and legislative overrides, but faces continued (if inconsistent) pushback. The US courts have been the primary resistance mechanism, issuing 39+ injunctions in the first five months, but the Supreme Court has increasingly sided with executive power.

3. **Personnel purges as mechanism:** Turkey purged 160,000+ civil servants post-2016. The US shed ~317,000 federal workers in 2025 (mostly voluntary but under coercive conditions including deferred resignation offers). India has used premature transfers and lateral entry to dilute institutional independence.

4. **Press freedom erosion:** Turkey (159th), India (157th), and the US (all declining) show convergent press freedom trajectories, though at vastly different levels. Turkey has imprisoned dozens of journalists; India has arrested reporters covering protests; the US has not reached that stage but has restricted media access.

## Key Differences

| Dimension | Turkey | India | United States |
|-----------|--------|-------|---------------|
| **Speed of degradation** | Gradual 2013-2016; accelerated massively after July 2016 coup | Incremental since 2014; continuous slow erosion | Unprecedented speed; 24% LDI decline in one year |
| **Primary mechanism** | Emergency decree powers + constitutional referendum | Legislative majoritarianism + executive action + appointment control | Executive orders + personnel mechanisms + agency dismantlement |
| **Institutional resistance** | Minimal after 2016 purge | Moderate (federalism, partial judicial independence) | Significant (courts, states, civil society) but eroding |
| **Statistical capture** | Near-complete (TurkStat) | Partial (suppression + methodology disputes) | Emerging (budget cuts + staff departures) |
| **Reversibility** | Low (decade of institutional destruction) | Moderate (federal structure preserves alternatives) | Uncertain (depends on court enforcement + 2028 elections) |
| **Freedom House 2026** | 32/100 (Not Free) | 62/100 (Partly Free) | 81/100 (Free, but declining) |
| **V-Dem classification** | Electoral Autocracy | Electoral Autocracy | Electoral Democracy (downgraded from Liberal Democracy) |
