Landscape — CHAI, the PEPFAR shock, and who does what in global health supply chains

CHAI was born from a procurement negotiation. In 2002, the Clinton Foundation's Ira Magaziner approached generic ARV manufacturers — Cipla, Ranbaxy, Aspen — and negotiated the first preferential pricing agreements for first-line antiretrovirals in developing countries. First-line ART fell from ~$15,000/person-year to ~$300 in under three years. This early success defined what CHAI fundamentally is: an organization with unusual access to manufacturer and government relationships, skilled at the specific kind of bilateral negotiation that produces price and access breakthroughs. The organization has grown to ~1,800 staff across 36 countries and ~$246M in annual revenue — but it remains at its core a market-shaping and government-embedding TA shop, not a technology company.

CHAI — what it has actually built (2002–2025)

Market shaping: ARV first-line prices from $15,000 to $45/person-year (2002–2023 arc); TDF reduction ~80% historically; ~$1.2B in DTG savings over seven years. These are CHAI's most unambiguous achievements, and none of them involve AI. They involve CHAI economists and country directors sitting with ministers and procurement officers and negotiating based on volume commitments and aggregate LMIC demand data.

Technical assistance: Embedded analysts in Ministries of Health in 30+ countries. Work includes quantification/forecasting (statistical methods, not ML), procurement planning, eLMIS/DHIS2 systems implementation, and distribution network redesign. CHAI also runs programs in TB detection, malaria commodity access, maternal/neonatal health, and vaccines financing — all non-AI.

Digital health: CHAI's Digital Health program helps governments implement EHR/DHIS2/eLMIS systems. This is digitization infrastructure — the foundation on which any analytics sits. CHAI does not operate a flagship deployed ML product with publicly documented accuracy metrics.

TSUs (2025): The newest program, triggered specifically by the PEPFAR funding shock. See below.

The PEPFAR / USAID collapse — scale and timing

The 2025 US foreign aid shock was the largest disruption to global health financing since the HIV crisis. Specific facts: PEPFAR's implementing-partner funding was cut by approximately 77% in the first wave (January–March 2025), with the Trump administration freezing most new obligations and terminating large portions of existing awards. By May 2025, UNAIDS documented >1,700 PEPFAR sites closed and >16,000 FTE laid off globally. GHSC-PSM — the USAID-funded supply chain management contractor (operated by Chemonics International) operating in 73 countries on a ~$1.15B budget — was terminated with no designated in-country logistics successor. VLS (viral load suppression) monitoring stopped in multiple countries. ART coverage was at risk in at minimum 15 countries with PEPFAR providing >50% of ARV financing.

The Global Fund 8th replenishment (late 2025) aimed to close part of the gap: the ask was $18B; the final outcome was $12.64B — approximately 30% short. Combined with the PEPFAR cuts, the total external health financing gap for HIV/TB/malaria programs is estimated in the $3–5B/yr range for 2025–2027.

Who does what in global health supply chains — the actual landscape

Understanding CHAI's position requires knowing who else is in the space. Before the 2025 cuts, the landscape was: USAID/GHSC-PSM (Chemonics; $1.15B/yr; logistics, commodity procurement, customs clearance, last-mile distribution in 73 countries — now terminated); Global Fund wambo.org (direct procurement platform for GF-funded commodities, handling ~$1.5B in commodity transactions/yr); UNICEF Supply Division (Copenhagen; largest global procurement agent for vaccines, RDTs, ORS, and other essential medicines; ~$3.5B/yr); CHAI (TA + market shaping + country-embedded advisors; does not do logistics); MSH (Management Sciences for Health; similar TA/advisory role to CHAI, USAID-funded, also cut heavily in 2025); PATH (received a TSU grant from GiveWell in parallel with CHAI April 2025).

The critical point: GHSC-PSM did logistics — warehousing, cold chain, customs, last-mile delivery. CHAI does not do this and cannot substitute for it. The USAID/PEPFAR shock removed the logistics and financing layer, not the advisory layer. CHAI's most valuable USAID-shock response is advisory (TSU fiscal triage), not supply chain logistics. The supply-chain AI narrative — that better forecasting will solve the post-USAID supply chain gap — is structurally wrong: perfect forecasts of unfunded shortfalls avert zero DALYs.

The AI framing problem — what CHAI does vs. what they call it

CHAI describes its supply-chain work using language that invokes machine learning, AI-driven forecasting, and algorithmic optimization. The description does not match the underlying technology. CHAI's HIV/TB/malaria forecasting tools are classical statistical instruments. The ARV "Simple Tool" is a morbidity-based forecasting spreadsheet. The global malaria commodity forecast is a Gates-funded statistical consortium (MAP/IVCC/RBM/MMV), not an AI product. CHAI operates at genuine scale — 29M people across 125 countries, 21M on HIV/TB/malaria medicines, ~$208M operating budget — but the "AI" descriptor attaches to a peripheral layer, not to the work doing the heavy lifting.

The best head-to-head evidence confirms the AI edge is thin even on its own terms. A 9-model bakeoff on Ethiopian essential-medicine demand at EPSS Gondar found XGBoost gave only "comparable model fitness with lower error estimates" vs. ARIMA and classical methods — a forecast-error improvement several causal links upstream of any averted death. Better forecast accuracy does not automatically translate to fewer stockouts; stockouts are driven primarily by funding constraints, procurement delays, supply-chain systems, and governance fragmentation, not by forecasting inaccuracy.

Why forecasting is not the binding constraint on stockouts — the systematic review evidence

A 2025 systematic review of stock-outs in HIV/TB/malaria programs identifies the dominant causes as: (1) funding gaps and cash-flow timing mismatches (the single most cited cause — no forecast fixes an unfunded shortfall); (2) procurement system delays and bureaucratic bottlenecks (tenders, supplier lead times); (3) weak supply-chain management capacity at subnational level; (4) governance fragmentation between central stores and implementing partners; and (5) manufacturing and supplier reliability issues. Inaccurate demand forecasting appears as a secondary or tertiary factor in most settings.

Post-USAID, the causal picture is even clearer: the binding constraint has shifted almost entirely to financing (commodity purchasing) and logistics capacity (no GHSC-PSM successor). Even perfect ML forecasting of a $3B annual shortfall produces zero averted DALYs if the commodities are not purchased. This is the structural flaw in the AI-supply-chain investment thesis as applied to the 2026 crisis context.

The flagship Zambia national supply-chain cluster-RCT (Vledder et al. 2019) measured availability of tracer drugs (process) with no mortality endpoint; it credits cross-docking network redesign, not ML. The multi-country eLMIS evaluation covering Guinea, Honduras, Rwanda, Tanzania (Reynolds 2021) reports reduced stockout odds (process) without linking to deaths. No independent study links CHAI forecasting analytics to averted deaths.

TSU fiscal-triage — the actual cost-effective work

CHAI's Technical Support Units (TSUs) embed fiscal and programmatic advisors in government health ministries in countries facing severe US funding cuts. The advisor's job: help government officials make better decisions about a shrinking health budget — which programs to protect, which domestic resources to reallocate, and how to make the case to the Global Fund or Gavi for emergency replenishment.

GiveWell's April 2025 grant ($4,657,500 across Burkina Faso, Malawi, Nigeria, Uganda, Zambia plus a global cross-cutting team) rates this work at 13–28× cash on a leverage basis. The leverage arithmetic: a ~$575k/country/yr TSU team enables a 12–14% cost-effectiveness improvement across a $20–40M government health expenditure envelope, plus additional efficiencies (4–30% waste reduction within existing spend) and crowded-in funding ($2–20M in external/domestic co-finance that would not otherwise flow to high-CE programs). Benefit assumed to accrue ~6 months beyond grant end.

TSU leverage mechanism. The critical dependency: the DALYs come from the envelope the TSU influences (government health budgets, GF proposals), not from the TA grant itself. Additionality is real but must be audited against domestic-spending displacement (Lu et al. estimate $0.14–0.43 displaced per $1 aid).

PATH also received a TSU grant — what this means for crowding

GiveWell also funded PATH's TSU (GiveWell PATH TSU, April 2025). This implies country-level crowding is possible — a CHAI TSU and a PATH TSU operating in the same country would have overlapping mandates. For an OAIF scale-out, require CHAI to coordinate with PATH (and MSH/other TSU providers) to avoid duplicate advisory in any single country. A simple "which countries are you already covering?" check before committing avoids this issue.

The displacement caveat — why 13–28× is an upper bound

The leverage framing carries a specific empirical risk: development assistance for health is observed to displace domestic government health spending. Lu et al. (PMC3348162) estimate that each $1 of external health aid displaces ~$0.14–0.43 of domestic government health spending; the estimate is contested and varies substantially by country (Rwanda: near-zero displacement; several lower-income SSA countries: high substitution effects).

If the TSU's budget-reallocation advice is partially offset by governments reducing domestic health commitments in response to the reallocated external funding, the net leverage multiple falls. The 13–28× GiveWell estimate assumes net additive reallocation — a reasonable but not verified assumption. A displacement audit (pre-registered tracking of domestic health budget trends against a synthetic control) should be a condition on Year 2 renewal.

Even with a 50% displacement rate, 6.5–14× cash is competitive with GiveWell top charities (~10× is the bar). The caveat matters for calibration; it does not reverse the recommendation.

Lenacapavir — the single strongest node in the CHAI portfolio

Lenacapavir (twice-yearly subcutaneous injectable PrEP) is the most efficacious HIV prevention intervention ever tested in a randomized trial. PURPOSE 1 (NEJM 2024): zero incident infections among 2,134 cisgender women in South Africa and Uganda who received lenacapavir — 100% efficacy vs. 16/1,000 person-years in the TDF/FTC arm. PURPOSE 2: 96% efficacy reduction vs. TDF/FTC in a global trial including men, transgender women, and people who inject drugs. These are not observational data — they are well-powered, double-blind RCTs with adjudicated endpoints. The evidence quality is qualitatively stronger than almost anything else in this portfolio.

Generic access is the only constraint. Gilead's originator list price is $28,218/person-year. CHAI's lenacapavir acceleration work — in partnership with Unitaid and Wits RHI — secured a generic access agreement in September 2025 targeting $40/person-year for LMICs by 2027, with Dr. Reddy's and Hetero as lead manufacturers. Lancet HIV modelling (Jamieson et al. 2024) finds lenacapavir averts 12.3–18% of HIV infections over 10 years in high-incidence settings, with $/DALY clearing <$500 at the $40/PY commodity floor.

Lenacapavir science — the capsid inhibitor mechanism and why it enables twice-yearly dosing

The capsid inhibitor mechanism

Lenacapavir is the first-in-class HIV capsid inhibitor, developed by Gilead Sciences and FDA-approved in 2022 (initially for treatment-experienced HIV in combination with other agents). Its target is the HIV-1 capsid protein (CA, p24), which assembles from ~1,500 monomers into pentamers and hexamers that form a conical lattice encapsulating the viral genome, reverse transcriptase, and integrase. This lattice protects the viral contents during cell entry and early replication steps.

LEN binds at a specific groove at the interface between hexamers — a hydrophobic pocket on the CA C-terminal domain that is also the binding site for the cellular cofactors CPSF6 and Nup153. By occupying this groove, LEN: (1) locks the capsid lattice in an abnormally stable conformation, preventing the normal disassembly ("uncoating") required for nuclear import and integration; (2) blocks the interaction with CPSF6 and nuclear pore machinery needed for nuclear transport; (3) at high concentrations, disrupts reverse transcription by preventing proper positioning of the RNA template within the capsid. The result is multi-stage inhibition — LEN acts during late stages of viral assembly in producer cells AND during early stages in newly infected cells, making resistance very difficult to select because mutations at the capsid hexamer interface typically compromise viral fitness severely.

Why the long half-life: LEN is highly lipophilic and binds extensively to plasma proteins and tissue. After subcutaneous injection into gluteal or abdominal tissue, it forms a depot that releases slowly. The terminal elimination half-life of ~50 days allows therapeutic plasma concentrations to be maintained for 6 months with a single 927mg subcutaneous dose (two injections at a single site). This half-life is fundamentally different from anything in existing PrEP (TDF/FTC daily oral; cabotegravir monthly or every 2-month injection). For populations where daily pill adherence is the barrier to PrEP access — adolescent girls, sex workers, people in unstable living situations — twice-yearly dosing removes the primary compliance obstacle.

Two honest caveats on the $100/DALY leverage estimate

Caveat 1 — The leverage framing credits CHAI with donor-financed DALYs

The ~$100/DALY leverage number credits CHAI with DALYs financed by PEPFAR, Global Fund, CIFF, and Gates commodity budgets — not by CHAI's own $2–4M grant. The calculation: CHAI's attributed 2-year acceleration of generic availability → X million person-years of earlier access → Y averted infections → Z DALYs per CHAI dollar. This is legitimate only if the 2-year acceleration is (a) real, (b) attributable to CHAI specifically, and (c) not double-counted across the crowd of accelerators.

The crowding is significant: Gilead signed direct licensing with six generic manufacturers in October 2024 in parallel with the CHAI/Unitaid pathway. The GF/PEPFAR coordinated effort targeting 2 million people on LEN-PrEP and a $912M Gates commitment were announced independently. CHAI's marginal acceleration contribution is difficult to isolate. A pre-registered counterfactual (when would generic registration have occurred without CHAI involvement in each country?) should be a condition on the grant.

Caveat 2 — $40/PY is a commodity floor, not a delivered cost

Real-world PrEP service delivery in Zambia ran $394–655/client-year (PMC10021804) — a 10–16× premium over the commodity price. Delivered costs include clinic visits, counseling, adherence support, and structural enablers that are essential for PrEP effectiveness. The full-cost $/DALY framing ($222/DALY median, P10 $142, P90 $359) is more honest than the leverage framing. Even $222/DALY is optimistic if real-world delivery costs approach Zambian levels; LEN's advantages (no daily adherence required) should reduce some delivery costs but not clinic infrastructure.

The defensible honest range: $100–250/DALY, additionality-gated, cash-competitive but not GiveWell top-charity class at current evidence. The RCT evidence is stronger than almost any other HIV prevention approach, which supports the upper end. The delivery-cost uncertainty pulls toward the lower end.

The USAID vacuum — what CHAI can and cannot backfill

The 2025 funding shock is structural, not cyclical. PEPFAR cut >77% of implementing partners; GHSC-PSM — the supply chain management backbone for 73 countries on $1.15B/yr — is terminating with no designated successor. The Global Fund 8th replenishment raised $12.64B vs. $18B ask (~30% short). The combined gap is in the $3–5B/yr range — roughly four to eight times CHAI's entire annual operating budget.

The vacuum is overwhelmingly commodities + physical logistics + frontline labor. CHAI's analytics layer cannot backfill any of these. A supply-chain AI that improves forecast accuracy is useless if the commodities aren't purchased, shipped, or managed by a functioning logistics system — all of which are the things PEPFAR/GHSC-PSM funded that are now absent. There is a real risk that funders experiencing acute guilt about the USAID collapse will fund "supply chain AI" as a technically-adjacent response to a crisis that requires commodity financing and logistics infrastructure. This would be expensive inaction dressed as action.

The one genuinely valuable CHAI response to the USAID shock — and the one that maps to what CHAI is actually skilled at — is the TSU fiscal-triage advisory: helping government health ministries navigate the worst of the cuts, identify which programs to protect first, and activate alternate financing. This is exactly the work GiveWell already rates at 13–28× cash. It also happens to have nothing to do with AI.

Grant structure — three tranches, kill-switched

Tranche 1 — TSU fiscal-triage scale-out $5–8M, 18–24 months

Expand the TSU model to 10–15 high-cut countries beyond GiveWell's current five (Burkina Faso, Malawi, Nigeria, Uganda, Zambia). First-tier additions: Ethiopia (large PEPFAR program, weak domestic health financing), Mozambique (most PEPFAR-dependent nation by share of total health spending), Kenya (large program, established CHAI relationships), Tanzania (similar). Coordinate with PATH to avoid duplicate advisory in the same country.

Staffing: $575k/country/yr × 10 countries = $5.75M for a 10-country expansion, consistent with absorptive capacity. Country teams: 3–4 FTE each, embedded in MoH, supported by the global cross-cutting unit.

Endpoint: $ of government/donor budget reallocated to higher-CE interventions, audited against a pre-registered counterfactual with domestic budget displacement tracking (Lu et al. methodology). Kill-switch: net reallocation <15% of TSU-advised budget at 18 months, OR displacement audit shows >50% substitution → do not renew. Require CHAI to report attributable reallocation by country against the GiveWell BOTEC benchmarks.

Tranche 2 — Lenacapavir acceleration/market-shaping $2–4M, one-time

Fund CHAI's market-shaping work on lenacapavir generic introduction: accelerating national regulatory registration timelines (EMA approved June 2025; country-by-country WHO-prequalification and national registration is the bottleneck), supporting health ministry formulary inclusion in high-incidence priority countries, and bridging financing for early rollout in high-incidence populations (sex workers, MSM, adolescent girls) where PEPFAR and GF funding is delayed.

Geographic prioritization: countries where CHAI has existing MoH relationships AND where HIV incidence remains >1% in the priority population AND where PEPFAR/GF pipeline is >6 months delayed. Likely top tier: South Africa (large adolescent girls pool, strong CHAI presence), Uganda, Zimbabwe, Mozambique, Tanzania.

Endpoint: months of generic availability acceleration vs. a pre-registered counterfactual (when would registration have occurred without CHAI involvement) + PrEP person-years initiated in target populations vs. a control-arm comparison. Kill-switch: if attribution CI includes zero acceleration (i.e., CHAI's involvement demonstrably didn't matter given Gilead's direct licensing) → decline renewal. Only fund high-incidence settings where $/DALY <$300 under conservative delivery-cost assumptions.

Tranche 3 (optional) — Supply-chain analytics HEAD-TO-HEAD ≤$3M, one-time

If a funder is genuinely uncertain whether there is an AI effect in supply-chain analytics (and OAIF may be, given the AI mandate): fund a pre-registered stepped-wedge or cluster-RCT comparing ML forecasting vs. classical statistical rules on verified stockout-days and treatment continuity (not forecast accuracy) at 12 months. This is the value-of-information calculation: $3M to resolve whether the supply-chain AI pathway is a $4,813 or $292/DALY proposition.

Kill-switch: if ML does not beat classical rules by a pre-specified margin at 12 months (CI lower bound excludes zero improvement in stockout-days) → publish the null and do not scale the AI layer. CHAI is an access, market-shaping, and government fiscal-capacity organization operating during the most acute global health financing crisis since the AIDS epidemic. Its supply-chain "AI" is peripheral classical analytics at $4,813–8,554/DALY — not cash-competitive, not GiveWell-class, and structurally unable to reach the $150/DALY threshold because forecasting accuracy is not what kills patients. The binding constraint is now commodity financing and logistics infrastructure — neither of which ML can provide.

Its cost-effective work — TSU fiscal-triage ($16/DALY, well inside threshold) and lenacapavir market-shaping ($100–222/DALY, conditionally inside threshold) — is exactly where there is no AI. Fund those as $7–15M in kill-switched tranches. Do not fund the AI premise. Do not fund open-ended scale without mortality measurement. Require displacement auditing on TSU and attribution verification on lenacapavir — both are tractable measurement problems that distinguish a $16/DALY grant from a $50/DALY or worse one.

The PEPFAR/USAID shock creates genuine additionality for the TSU work: governments that just lost major bilateral funding partners need exactly the kind of fiscal-triage capacity CHAI's TSU model provides. That is the legitimate additionality story. It has nothing to do with machine learning.
