Cause Area Report · September 2026

Microsporidia MB

A tiny, naturally occurring microbe already living inside malaria-carrying mosquitoes in Kenya appears to make those mosquitoes completely unable to transmit malaria — and it spreads on its own through mosquito populations. The team behind it (SYMBIOVECTOR, run out of Africa's leading insect research institute) is asking for $50M over five years to prove it works in the real world and deploy it across three countries.
Bottom line

Fund the first phase (~$9M) now. Reserve the second phase (~$24M) until the first phase shows the delivery method actually works. Don't commit to the third phase ($17M) until there's real-world data from a field release. The science is genuinely impressive for something this early — 37,879 wild mosquitoes tested over 30 months show the microbe cuts malaria transmission by roughly half. But the gap between "works in mosquito cages" and "works in wild populations after a one-time release" has never been crossed, and our modelling suggests it's harder to cross than the proposal implies.

If it works: this could protect millions of people from malaria at $47 per year of healthy life — competitive with bednets, but without needing to re-buy or re-distribute anything after the initial release. It would also reach the people bednets miss: those bitten by mosquitoes outdoors.

If the field release fails: we're out ~$15–30M, the programme either pivots or ends, and the field learns something important. The downside is bounded and the upside is enormous, which is why this is a reasonable bet at the right stage size.

The idea — what it is and why it's different

Malaria is spread by mosquitoes. A mosquito bites someone with malaria, the parasite grows inside the mosquito for a couple of weeks, and then when the mosquito bites someone else, it injects the parasite into them. Every existing tool works by interrupting this somewhere: bednets stop the bite, indoor spraying kills the mosquito, drugs treat the person after infection. All of these have to be bought and distributed every year. And mosquitoes keep evolving resistance to the insecticides, so the tools keep getting weaker.

Microsporidia MB takes a different approach. It's a tiny, naturally occurring microbe — essentially a fungal parasite of mosquitoes — that was discovered living inside wild mosquitoes near Lake Victoria in Kenya in 2018. When a mosquito carries this microbe, the malaria parasite can't complete its development inside the mosquito. The mosquito bites as normal, but it can no longer pass malaria on. The block isn't partial — infected mosquitoes appear to be completely unable to transmit malaria.

The important part is how the microbe spreads. It passes from a mother mosquito to her offspring (on average about 74% of the time), and also spreads between adult mosquitoes when they mate. This means once you get enough of it into a wild population, the microbe can spread and hold its position without any further action — no re-spraying, no re-buying, no annual campaign. The idea is a one-time seeding that then runs itself.

The innovation in the programme's September 2026 concept note is the delivery method. Rather than releasing infected adult mosquitoes (which is expensive and politically tricky), the team has found that the microbe produces spores that can infect tiny water crustaceans called Moina. These crustaceans live naturally in mosquito breeding sites — stagnant water, irrigation channels, rice paddies — and when infected, they release a form of the spore that's highly infectious to mosquito larvae growing in the same water. So the plan is: produce spores centrally, distribute them to breeding sites, and let the crustaceans do the amplification. No mosquito releases needed.

The closest thing to this that's worked in the real world is Wolbachia, a different microbe used to stop dengue transmission. The Wolbachia programme now protects 16 million people in 15 countries. MB is attempting to do the same thing for malaria.

The open questions — what decides whether this works

The central question: will one release be enough, or does it need topping up forever?

Here's the problem in plain terms. In a mosquito cage, MB reaches ~80% of mosquitoes and stays there without any further seeding — it spreads on its own. But in wild Kenyan mosquito populations, MB naturally sits at around 13% and never tips over to high levels on its own. That gap is the central unanswered question.

Our modelling of the underlying population dynamics suggests why: for the microbe to permanently take over a wild population, you need to seed enough infected mosquitoes at once to cross a tipping point — above which the microbe spreads faster than it fades, below which it just dies back. We estimate that tipping point at roughly 39% of the adult mosquito population in a given area. Our models also suggest that after a single release, the microbe self-sustains permanently (rather than eventually fading) in only about 1 in 80 scenarios under realistic conditions.

This is the big difference from Wolbachia/dengue, which has a much lower tipping point (~20–30%) and locks in more easily. Wolbachia has a biological mechanism (it makes uninfected females partially sterile when they mate with infected males) that actively helps it take over. MB doesn't have this — it spreads more slowly and needs more help to cross the threshold.

The concept note is optimistic about this — projecting six years of protection from two spore releases. The truth is we don't know yet, which is exactly why the field release (Stage 2) is the most important thing the programme will do.

Does it actually self-sustain in the other mosquito species?

The concept note claims MB blocks malaria transmission in multiple mosquito species, including An. funestus, which has become the dominant malaria-carrier across much of East and Southern Africa. If that's true, the programme's potential reach roughly doubles. If MB can only spread and sustain itself in the species it was originally found in (An. arabiensis), the reach is significantly smaller and the cost per person protected is higher.

The subtlety here: "blocking transmission in a lab test" is not the same as "spreading through a wild population on its own." A 2021 study in Ghana found MB in 0 out of 181 An. funestus specimens tested. The concept note claims evidence of blocking across species but doesn't clarify whether this means the microbe can also pass from mother to offspring in those species. That distinction matters enormously for whether the multi-species reach claim holds up.

The field release — what it involves and what we'd learn

The Stage 2 field release at Ahero in western Kenya is the pivotal moment. It's the first time anyone will try to seed MB into a real wild mosquito population and see if it takes hold.

Based on our modelling of the population dynamics, the release needs to be substantial enough to cross the tipping point — roughly 39% of the adult mosquito population in the target area infected. At typical wild mosquito densities, that means releasing around 10,000 infected mosquitoes per hectare, per round, over 4–6 rounds spread across a 10–14 week campaign. The release should be timed for the dry season, when mosquito numbers are low and a given number of released mosquitoes represents a larger fraction of the total. Total mosquitoes needed: roughly 1–6 million. Our estimated cost for the Ahero pilot alone: about $1.9M.

The key question the programme hasn't answered publicly: is their planned release designed around this threshold? If they release fewer mosquitoes than needed to cross the tipping point, the experiment will show MB fading away — but that might be a design failure rather than evidence that MB can't work. Getting the design right is as important as doing the release.

What Stage 2 would tell us:

  • If MB levels stay high after release: the self-sustaining mechanism works in the wild. The programme moves to Stage 3 with strong evidence and the cost model ($36–47/DALY at scale) becomes credible.
  • If MB levels fade back to the natural ~13%: the "pay once" model doesn't work. The programme either needs to develop a stronger biological drive mechanism (a bigger scientific challenge) or pivot to treating MB like a recurring pesticide application (which changes the economics entirely).

Will it last? The malaria parasite won't just give up

A fair question: if MB blocks malaria in mosquitoes, can the malaria parasite eventually evolve a way around it — the same way mosquitoes evolved resistance to the insecticides in bednets?

Our dedicated research loop on this found that resistance is very unlikely, and structurally so — not just lucky. Three reasons:

  1. The bottleneck is tiny. For the malaria parasite to evolve resistance, a resistant variant needs to arise by random mutation at exactly the right moment. In a human body, there are hundreds of billions of malaria parasites — plenty of chances for mutations. But inside a mosquito, the parasite population shrinks to just 1–20 individuals at the stage where MB kills it. That's a billion-fold fewer opportunities for resistance to arise. The math strongly disfavours it.
  2. The block works through multiple mechanisms at once. MB doesn't just block one step in the malaria parasite's development — it appears to trigger a broad change in the mosquito's immune state, gut chemistry, and microbiome simultaneously. For the parasite to escape, it would need to overcome all of these at once, which is far harder than evolving past a single chemical or protein.
  3. The historical track record. Every known case of a microbe blocking a pathogen inside an insect — including Wolbachia blocking dengue — has stayed effective. There is no documented case of a pathogen evolving around this kind of symbiont-based block, even under deliberately hostile laboratory conditions.

Our modelling put numbers on this. Even under pessimistic assumptions, the probability of malaria developing resistance to MB within 10 years is roughly 1–17%. The median time for resistance to appear, even generously assuming it's possible, is around 277 years. Compare that to pyrethroids — the insecticide in most bednets — where resistance in the exact region where MB would be deployed rose from near-zero to near-complete in about 9 years.

In short: MB's durability advantage over insecticides is enormous. Resistance risk should not weigh heavily against this investment.

How many people could it realistically protect?

The programme's own estimate projects coverage across much of Africa's malaria belt — areas where temperatures are right for MB to thrive and mosquito populations are large enough to matter. That's a reasonable long-term vision.

Near-term, the picture is more constrained. The Lake Victoria basin (where Ahero is) has the right mosquito species and is the natural starting point, but roughly 60% of malaria transmission there comes from mosquito species other than the one MB has been most studied in. That means the near-term protected population is smaller than the headline addressable numbers suggest, and the near-term cost per person protected is higher.

The near-term number ($164) is 2–3× above the typical benchmark for highly cost-effective global health programmes (~$50–100). The investment case isn't that the pilot is cost-effective on its own — it's that the pilot unlocks a credible path to ~$36 at East African scale, which is extremely cost-effective. A funder who commits through to the East African phase would see a blended average across the whole programme of roughly $27–34 per year of healthy life.

One important caveat on reach: MB appears to spread best within a specific temperature range. The concept note shows this range coincides with a large fraction of Africa's malaria burden. But during the hottest months, prevalence drops in semi-field conditions. The programme hasn't yet quantified exactly which areas fall outside the optimal range during which parts of the year.

How to stage the funding

The programme's concept note proposes three stages. Here's what each one actually does and what we think the right commitment structure is.

Stage 1 · Years 1–2
Prove the delivery method works; build the alliance
~$9M

The primary job of Stage 1 is confirming that the spore/crustacean delivery method — the innovation that makes this whole programme cheaper and more tractable than alternatives — actually works at field-relevant scale. This hasn't been demonstrated yet. Stage 1 also sets up a 10-country scientific network across Africa (Cameroon, Côte d'Ivoire, Ethiopia, Madagascar, Nigeria, Tanzania, Uganda, plus the three pilot countries) for data sharing and capacity building, and begins regulatory conversations in Kenya, Rwanda, and Benin.

This stage resolves
Can we actually produce and deliver MB spores at the scale needed for a field release? Is the crustacean amplification step reliable?
Stop funding if
The spore delivery system cannot be shown to work at field-relevant production volumes. Redirect remaining budget to the funestus-blocking experiment instead.
Stage 2 · Years 2–4
The field release — does it work in the wild?
~$24M

Stage 2 runs pilot releases in Kenya, Rwanda, and Benin simultaneously rather than one after another. The parallel approach generates evidence across three different ecological and regulatory contexts at once, and means the programme isn't hostage to any single country's approval process. Kenya is the R&D hub (where MB was discovered); Rwanda adds a second pathway through a government known for fast adoption of health innovations and serves as a gateway to the high-burden Great Lakes region; Benin establishes a West African hub for eventual expansion into countries like Nigeria and Côte d'Ivoire.

The actual pilot release at Ahero that resolves the central question would cost roughly $1.9M by our independent estimate. The larger $24M covers manufacturing infrastructure, three parallel regulatory processes, and monitoring across all three countries.

Does MB take hold in wild populations after a deliberate release, or does it fade back? This is the question the entire investment case turns on.
Stop funding if
MB consistently fades back to background levels after a well-designed release in multiple sites. This would mean the "pay once" model doesn't work and the programme needs fundamental redesign.
Stage 3 · Year 4+
Build demonstration sites; hand off to national programmes
~$17M

Stage 3 isn't about proving MB works — that's Stage 2's job. It's about creating "demonstration landscapes": real communities where MB is established and running at operational scale, which governments, national malaria programmes, and other funders can visit, evaluate, and decide to replicate using their existing budgets. The long-term goal is for MB to be funded through redirected malaria-control budgets (which currently pay for bednets, spraying, and drugs every year) rather than requiring new philanthropic capital indefinitely.

Our recommended structure: Fund Stage 1 now. Also fund the funestus-blocking experiment (~$1–2M) as a separate standalone — it's the cheapest question that most changes the cost estimate. Reserve Stage 2 funding (~$24M) without disbursing, conditional on Stage 1 delivery confirmation. Commit to Stage 3 only after Stage 2 field data exists.

Cost-effectiveness — what the numbers actually mean

Cost-effectiveness in global health is measured in dollars per DALY — "disability-adjusted life year." One DALY is roughly one year of healthy life lost to illness or premature death. A programme costs $50/DALY if spending $50 prevents one year of healthy life being lost. GiveWell's rough benchmark for "highly cost-effective" is around $50–100/DALY.

The probability-weighted number looks bad, but it's the right framing for a research bet at this stage — it's comparable to how we'd evaluate a drug candidate in early trials. The point isn't that the expected value is guaranteed to be good; it's that if it works, the payoff is large enough to justify a staged bet, and if it doesn't, the losses are bounded and the field learns something.

How it compares to what we're already doing

Bednets and indoor spraying

The incumbent tools. GiveWell estimates roughly $50–100 per year of healthy life protected at the margin. MB near-term is $164/DALY — worse than bednets — but has two structural advantages bednets don't: it's potentially self-sustaining (once established, cost drops close to zero), and it reaches outdoor biting, which accounts for roughly half of transmission in the Lake Victoria region and which bednets simply can't reach regardless of how many people use them.

Wolbachia for dengue — the closest analogy

Wolbachia is a different microbe that blocks dengue transmission in Aedes mosquitoes. It's been deployed in 15 countries and now protects 16 million people from dengue, at around $1 per person at scale. The World Mosquito Program spent roughly 15 years and hundreds of millions of dollars developing it. MB is at a comparable stage to where Wolbachia was about 10–15 years ago.

The key difference: Wolbachia has a biological property that helps it spread aggressively through wild populations (it causes partial infertility in uninfected females, punishing the uninfected). MB doesn't have this — it spreads more slowly and needs a higher initial seeding density to take hold. This is why the field release is harder and less certain for MB than it was for Wolbachia.

Gene drives

Gene drives are genetic modifications designed to spread through mosquito populations and either suppress them or make them unable to transmit malaria. They've attracted significant attention but face major unresolved technical hurdles (the drives keep breaking down in lab populations), and their regulatory and public-acceptance challenges have proven harder than expected. MB's advantage is that it's naturally occurring — it's a microbe already present in these mosquito populations — which makes the regulatory path much clearer and public acceptance higher. MB could be deployable in 2–4 years; gene drives are realistically 10+ years away.

How it fits the broader portfolio

MB is a different kind of bet from the more immediate global health investments in the portfolio (bednets, direct cash, treatment programmes). Those are high-confidence, lower-upside; MB is low-confidence, very high-upside if it works. They serve different purposes and don't compete for the same capital. A reasonable framing: ~60–70% of philanthropy dollars in deployable, proven programmes; ~10–15% in a staged option like MB that pays off massively if the field release succeeds.

The four questions that would change our view

These are the questions whose answers would change whether — and how much — to fund. Not just interesting gaps: things that would shift the verdict. Several obvious questions are already answered by the concept note (see below).

What we'd want to know before committing Stage 2

  1. Is the spore/crustacean delivery method confirmed in the lab, or is it still theoretical?

    The concept note describes this as the delivery plan and cites a couple of references. But it also says that at-scale production is "expected to combine" the two methods — the word "expected" is a flag. If the full spore→crustacean→mosquito-larvae infectious chain hasn't been confirmed beyond preliminary experiments, Stage 1 might not succeed at its own primary goal. We'd want to know: has this chain been shown to work end-to-end in a lab setting? What's the current production rate and how far is it from what a field release would need?

  2. Can the microbe spread from mother to offspring in the funestus mosquito species — or does it just block transmission without spreading?

    The concept note says MB blocks malaria in multiple species including An. funestus, which now dominates transmission across much of East Africa. But there's a crucial difference between "blocks transmission in a lab test" and "can spread through a wild funestus population on its own." A 2021 study in Ghana found MB in zero out of 181 funestus mosquitoes tested. If MB can only transiently infect funestus (block it once but not pass to offspring), the programme's reach is significantly smaller and the cost-per-person-protected roughly doubles. Has inheritance in funestus been demonstrated, and in what experiment?

  3. Is Stage 2's release designed around the tipping point — or is it likely to be too small to answer the question?

    Our modelling of wild mosquito population dynamics suggests MB needs to be seeded in roughly 39% of the adult population at once before it spreads on its own rather than fading back. At typical densities, that means releasing around 10,000 infected mosquitoes per hectare per round, over 4–6 rounds. If the programme's Stage 2 plan uses lower densities or a single release, the experiment might show MB fading back — not because MB can't work, but because the design wasn't ambitious enough. Spending $24M on a poorly-designed experiment that can't answer the question it's supposed to answer is the specific risk to avoid. What is their planned release size, and has it been chosen with this threshold in mind?

  4. If the field release fails, what happens next — does the programme end, or pivot to something else?

    The concept note doesn't address this. If Stage 2 shows that MB fades back to low levels after release, the two options are: (a) try to add a stronger biological driver (essentially becoming a gene-drive programme, which is a much bigger scientific challenge), or (b) treat MB as a recurring-application tool like a pesticide, which means the economics look more like $230+/DALY than $47/DALY. The answer changes how to structure the Stage 2 commitment. If there's a viable Plan B that would still be worth pursuing, that changes the downside calculation. If the answer is "the programme ends," that's also fine — but it should be stated explicitly so the kill-gate is clear.

Already answered — no need to ask

Questions the concept note already addresses
  • Does MB block malaria in funestus at all? Yes — the concept note claims "evidence that MB blocks transmission in multiple vector species including An. funestus." Our open question above is specifically about whether it can also spread through funestus populations on its own, which is different.
  • Is the spore/crustacean route the delivery plan? Yes — explicitly described in the concept note with references. Our question is about how far the development is, not whether they intend to use it.
  • Why three countries at once rather than one at a time? Answered: running three regulatory and implementation pathways in parallel reduces the risk of being stuck if one country's process stalls, and generates evidence across three different ecological contexts faster.
  • Why those three countries specifically? Kenya is where MB was discovered and has the most scientific infrastructure; Rwanda has a government known for fast health innovation adoption and is a gateway to the high-burden Great Lakes region; Benin gives a foothold in West Africa and a path to expansion in French-speaking countries.
  • Who else is funding this? Open Philanthropy and the Gates Foundation have both funded Phases 1 and 2. Phase 1 also included funding from the Children's Investment Fund Foundation. Combined investment to date: ~$12M.
  • Is the community likely to accept this? Yes — the concept note cites research establishing high public acceptance in western Kenya. The spore-seeding approach (no mosquito releases) appears to increase acceptance further compared to programmes that release mosquitoes.
  • Is it classified as a GMO? The microbe is naturally occurring, not genetically engineered, which puts it on a clearer regulatory path. The Wolbachia/dengue programme has already navigated this classification in multiple countries. Country-specific legal status is worth confirming but isn't expected to be a fundamental barrier.

Verdict

This is a genuine bet, not a sure thing. After 10 research loops across two phases, the honest picture is: the underlying biology is real and impressive, the delivery innovation is clever, the resistance risk is low, and the potential scale is enormous — but the single most important question (does it actually work in the wild?) has never been tested, and our modelling suggests it's harder than the programme implies.

The case for funding isn't certainty. It's this:

  • The malaria-blocking mechanism is real, confirmed in nearly 38,000 wild mosquitoes over 30 months. This isn't a lab curiosity.
  • The new delivery approach — spreading spores through water crustaceans rather than releasing mosquitoes — makes Stage 2 tractable and inexpensive compared to anything similar that's been tried.
  • If Stage 2 works, the programme becomes one of the most cost-effective malaria interventions available, at a fraction of the cost of bednets on a long-run basis, reaching populations bednets can't protect.
  • If Stage 2 fails, the programme exits at $15–30M total spent, the science community learns something important, and the portfolio is otherwise unaffected.
  • The Stage 1 ($9M) + funestus experiment ($1–2M) together are the cheapest way to dramatically reduce uncertainty before committing to Stage 2.

Recommended action: Fund Stage 1 (~$9M) now. Separately fund the funestus-inheritance experiment (~$1–2M) — it's the cheapest single question that most changes the cost estimate. Hold Stage 2 (~$24M) in reserve, to be co-funded with Open Philanthropy and Gates, conditional on Stage 1 confirming the delivery method works. Don't commit Stage 3 ($17M) until the Stage 2 field data exists.

The cost of waiting matters here. The biggest delays in global health typically happen not during the science, but in the gap between "it works in the lab" and "someone commits to finding out if it works in the real world." Every year MB waits for the next funding cycle rather than running Stage 2 is a year of potential malaria burden not addressed. The downside of funding and being wrong is at most $30M. The downside of not funding and being right is measured in lives.
