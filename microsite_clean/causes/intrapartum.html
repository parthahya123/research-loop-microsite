Intrapartum Sensing & POCUS

About 2 million babies die every year at or just after birth — most from preventable complications during labor. The technology to detect these complications already exists and is cheap. The problem is that detecting danger doesn't save babies if the facility can't act on it.

Verdict

Fund SBBC scale-up ($144/DALY, measured) and an AI-CTG replication RCT ($6–12M). For the Gates Bihar hub-and-spoke proposal: fund the $5M measurement tranche now, gate $15M on a 12-month conversion rate, and hold $30M for 24-month outcome data. Do not commit the full $50M upfront.

The Gates proposal's 250M DALY headline is implausible by 3–50× as a central estimate. The honest expected value is dominated by a ~60–80% probability of null effect — the INFANT trial (47,000 women, aRR 1.01) is the most relevant prior. The hub-and-spoke mechanism is genuinely different and worth testing, but it is untested. Operational metrics at 12 months can gate the $15M tranche; they cannot substitute for the outcome data the $30M tranche requires.

The binding constraint is response capacity — Bihar has 39% anaesthetist coverage and a 2.7% C-section rate. HBB training at $12–23/DALY addresses that constraint directly and is proven. Any AI-CTG investment is a bet that a remote OB hub overrides it.

The problem

Every year, roughly 2 million babies die during or immediately after birth — about 1 million stillbirths plus 900,000 neonatal deaths in the first 24 hours. The leading cause is birth asphyxia: the baby doesn't get enough oxygen during labor. Most of these deaths happen in low- and middle-income countries at facilities that have some staff and some equipment, but not enough of either.

The sequence of events is usually the same: the baby's heart rate changes in ways that signal distress. If that signal is detected and acted on fast enough — by speeding up delivery, performing an emergency cesarean, or resuscitating the baby at birth — the baby survives. If not, it doesn't.

The obvious solution sounds simple: better monitoring so the distress signal is caught earlier. That's the pitch behind most of the technology in this space. The problem, as we'll explain, is that catching the signal is not actually the hard part.

The core insight that changes everything

Here is the single most important finding from all of our research on this topic:

Detecting fetal distress does not save babies. Acting on fetal distress does. Those are two different problems, and the second one is much harder.

The best evidence for this comes from a rigorous study of the Moyo fetal heart rate monitor in Tanzania — a continuous, purpose-built device that genuinely does detect distress better and faster than the alternatives. In a study of nearly 3,000 women, Moyo detected abnormal heart rate patterns significantly more often than standard care. C-section rates went up. But deaths did not go down.

What happened? The facilities detected more danger signals, but their capacity to respond — emergency surgery, trained staff, time — didn't change. Detecting danger and not being able to act on it doesn't help the baby.

This pattern recurs throughout the literature. The facilities where intrapartum death rates are highest are exactly the facilities where response capacity is weakest. A better monitor is upstream of the bottleneck.

Bet 1 — SBBC scale-up (the clear opportunity)

The Safer Births Bundle of Care is not a single device. It is four things deployed together:

  1. Simulation training — midwives practice resuscitation and emergency deliveries on realistic mannequins
  2. Continuous quality improvement — facilities track outcomes, audit deaths, and share learning across sites
  3. Clinical devices — Moyo (continuous fetal heart rate monitor), NeoBeat (newborn heart rate), suction and bag-mask for resuscitation
  4. Sustainability systems — government integration, local ownership, mentorship networks

This is the only intervention in the intrapartum space that attacks both the detection problem and the response problem at once. Training improves what staff do when they see a distress signal. QI systems create accountability for outcomes. The equipment is cheap and purpose-built for LMIC settings.

What the evidence shows

A 2025 NEJM study across 30 facilities in Tanzania (281,000 mothers) found:

  • 18% reduction in deaths in the first 24 hours after birth
  • 40% reduction in early newborn deaths
  • 75% reduction in maternal deaths in hospital
  • ~580 lives saved at those 30 facilities

A companion cost-effectiveness analysis found $144/DALY — competitive with GiveWell's top interventions and a benefit-cost ratio of 36.

Important caveat
The Tanzania study used a stepped-wedge design, not a randomized controlled trial. That means facilities switched over sequentially and there was no pure control group. Secular trends and the Hawthorne effect (staff performing better because they're being observed) cannot be ruled out. The effect could be smaller than the headline figure. The Nigeria expansion (now underway) is the first real replication test — its results, expected 2027–28, should gate major scale-up commitments.

At $144/DALY (the measured rate) and $80–150k per facility:

Priority countries by burden: India, Nigeria, DRC, Ethiopia, Pakistan together account for over half of global intrapartum deaths.

Bet 2 — AI-assisted CTG interpretation (the speculative bet)

A cardiotocograph, or CTG, is the squiggly line that traces a baby's heart rate during labor. Reading it well requires training — you have to recognize which patterns signal distress and which are normal variation. The pitch for AI-CTG is: most LMIC facilities don't have a specialist who can read this trace well, so an AI that reads it for them could save lives.

The pitch makes logical sense. The problem is that it doesn't seem to work in practice — and we have unusually strong evidence for why.

The INFANT trial — the decisive test

The INFANT trial randomized 47,000 women across 24 hospitals in the UK and Ireland to labor with or without a real-time AI system that reads the CTG trace and alerts clinicians to danger patterns. This is the largest trial ever run of this type of technology, in hospitals with skilled staff, good equipment, and strong response capacity. The result: deaths occurred in 0.7% of the AI group and 0.7% of the control group. No difference at all. C-section rates didn't improve either.

Why? Because even in those hospitals, the problem wasn't that clinicians were missing the signals — it was that the chain from signal to action was slower and more complex than the AI could fix. This is the response-capacity problem in a well-resourced setting. In an under-resourced LMIC facility, the same problem is much worse.

What about the Malawi result?

One study in Malawi reported a 75–82% reduction in intrapartum deaths with an AI-CTG system (PeriGen). That number is striking. But it comes from a single before-after comparison at one unusually well-resourced hospital with 24-hour obstetrician coverage — the opposite of a typical LMIC setting. It has not been replicated anywhere.

The accuracy problem

When AI-CTG systems are tested on how accurately they read traces, they perform reasonably well at identifying abnormal patterns. But the accuracy improvement is mainly on the sensitivity axis (catching real danger). The specificity (not falsely flagging normal babies as in danger) is stubbornly around 60% — meaning a lot of false alarms, which drive unnecessary C-sections and erode trust in the system.

So what is AI-CTG actually useful for?

Two things appear genuinely valuable, independent of the interpretation claim:

  • Signal acquisition — battery-powered, beltless devices like the Janitri Keyar (see below) that can capture a fetal heart trace in settings without the power, consumables, and infrastructure of a conventional CTG machine. This is a real hardware advance.
  • Telemonitoring — streaming the trace to a remote specialist who can read it. A scarce obstetrician in a district hub could supervise multiple rural labor wards simultaneously. This is valuable where connectivity is reliable.

The interpretation claim — AI substituting for the absent expert — is the part that hasn't been proven and may not work for the reasons the INFANT trial exposed.

What would it take to settle the question?

A rigorous test would need to be a cluster randomized trial in LMIC facilities, with outcomes (deaths, encephalopathy, appropriate vs. inappropriate C-sections) as the primary measure — not trace-reading accuracy. It would need to compare against the real counterfactual: a birth attendant with no CTG training, not a specialist. It would need to separately test the acquisition, interpretation, and telemonitoring legs, so you can tell which part (if any) is driving the effect. Estimated cost: $6–12M. If the effect replicates, AI-CTG could become a $2–8/DALY intervention. If it doesn't, the loss is contained.

The Gates proposal — what's actually being asked

In October 2026, the Gates Foundation MNCH team brought a specific proposal to potential co-funders. Understanding it requires separating three things: what is being built, why they think it will work, and what the evidence actually says.

What is being proposed

Gates wants to deploy AI-assisted fetal monitoring across hundreds of public hospitals in India's poorest states — primarily Bihar, Uttar Pradesh, Assam, and Meghalaya. The specific device (Janitri's Keyar) attaches patches to a laboring woman's abdomen, reads her baby's heart rate continuously, and streams that data to a central dashboard. An obstetrician sitting at that dashboard — potentially in a city hundreds of kilometers away — watches multiple labor wards simultaneously. When the AI flags a baby in distress, the remote OB reviews the trace and decides whether to call for immediate action: speed up the delivery, perform an emergency C-section, or send an ambulance to transfer the mother to a better-equipped hospital.

This is called a "hub-and-spoke" model. One expert hub. Many peripheral spokes. The implied pitch: India doesn't have enough obstetricians in rural areas, so put them somewhere central and use technology to extend their reach.

The briefing's headline claim: this approach could avert 250 million DALYs — a figure presented as evidence that this is the highest-impact product in Gates' entire MNCH portfolio.

Why the 250 million DALY number is wrong

We independently modeled this figure. It doesn't survive basic arithmetic.

Start with the total global pool of harm this intervention could possibly address: roughly 85 million DALYs per year from intrapartum complications (birth asphyxia deaths, brain damage in survivors, and intrapartum stillbirths). To accumulate 250 million DALYs over 20 years — at 60% coverage — the program would need to eliminate at least 25% of all intrapartum harm globally. The best randomized trial ever run on this class of intervention found it eliminated exactly zero percent.

That trial — the INFANT trial, 47,000 women across 24 hospitals in the UK — tested AI-assisted CTG interpretation in hospitals with skilled staff, reliable power, and functioning operating theaters. The result: deaths occurred at the same rate in both groups. If AI-CTG has zero effect in a well-resourced setting, the 250M DALY figure requires it to have a 25%+ effect in one of India's most under-resourced health systems. That is not a central estimate. It is a best-case ceiling that requires every assumption to go right simultaneously.

At realistic effect sizes, the number collapses by orders of magnitude. Even being generous — a 10% effect, which would make this more effective than any RCT of this technology has ever shown — the 20-year cumulative impact for the India program specifically is roughly 10 million DALYs, not 250 million. The headline is off by 25×.

Why hub-and-spoke might still be worth investigating

Here is the honest case for the program, stated fairly.

The INFANT trial tested something specific: giving an AI system to the same clinician who was already reading the CTG. The AI gave advice; the clinician could take it or leave it. Hub-and-spoke is structurally different. Instead of advising a local clinician, it brings in a senior obstetrician who has authority to override local judgment and direct a course of action — transfer this patient now, call the anesthesiologist, prep the theater. That is closer to a telemedicine consultation than a decision-support tool.

If the remote OB genuinely can direct emergency management effectively — and if the peripheral facility has even minimal capacity to execute those instructions — the hub-and-spoke model bypasses the binding constraint that killed the INFANT trial. It's not just better software; it's a scarce expert being deployed more efficiently.

That's a real and interesting hypothesis. The problem is that it is completely untested. No randomized trial has ever evaluated hub-and-spoke AI-CTG in an LMIC setting. All the encouraging before-after data comes from studies too weak to be trusted — and we know this because before-after AI-CTG data in the UK also looked encouraging before the INFANT RCT showed it was wrong.

The infrastructure problem

Even if the hub works perfectly — the AI flags distress, the remote OB sees it immediately, calls the peripheral facility, and orders an emergency delivery — the chain then hits a hard wall.

What Bihar's hospitals actually look like
Only 39% of Bihar's referral hospitals have an on-site anesthesiologist — which means 61% cannot perform an emergency C-section at all. The median response time for an emergency ambulance is 60–90 minutes. Among women who are transferred in emergency obstetric situations, 8.4% deliver before they reach the receiving hospital. Bihar's public C-section rate is 2.7–3.6%, against a WHO-estimated unmet need of ~10%. The operating rooms exist on paper; the people to staff them often don't.

When the hub OB says "this baby needs to be delivered now," what actually happens at the spoke? At 61% of facilities, a C-section is not possible regardless of the decision. The only option is to call an ambulance, load the mother, and hope she reaches a better hospital in time. That is a 60–90 minute window for a baby in documented acute distress.

This is not a reason to abandon the program. It is a reason to know, before committing $50M, whether the hub-and-spoke conversion rate — the fraction of alerts that actually result in a timely clinical action — is high enough to move the needle on outcomes. That number is currently unmeasured.

Can we structure the funding to reduce risk without waiting years for an RCT?

This is the right question, and the answer is: partially. You can reduce risk on the first $20M by gating on operational metrics at 12 months. You cannot reduce risk on the final $30M without outcome data — and that takes 18–24 months regardless of what else you do.

The core problem with process metrics

The instinct is to gate on things you can measure quickly: Is the system being used? Are providers acting on alerts? Is the hub responding fast enough? These feel like leading indicators of whether the program is working.

The INFANT trial is a direct counterargument. In INFANT, providers used the AI guidance at high rates. They acted on alerts quickly. They escalated appropriately. Every operational metric looked good. The RCT showed zero effect on deaths. Process metrics passed; outcomes failed.

Why? Because detecting a problem and successfully acting on it are two different things. The CTG showed distress. The AI flagged it. The doctor saw the flag. The doctor ordered a C-section. The C-section happened. The baby died anyway — because the asphyxia was already too far advanced, or the resuscitation was inadequate, or the baby had an underlying condition the CTG couldn't detect. The whole chain executed correctly and the outcome was still null.

The same risk exists in Bihar. The hub flags distress. The local provider calls an ambulance. The ambulance comes. The mother delivers en route. The chain executed. The outcome was not good.

What you can and can't gate on

Not all process metrics are equally useless. The most predictive one for this program is the conversion rate: of all the cases the hub OB flags as "urgent action required," what fraction result in a documented clinical action — an emergency C-section, instrumental delivery, or transfer initiated — within 60 minutes? This goes beyond mere detection. It measures whether the hub's expert decision is actually being executed at the spoke. A conversion rate below 20% almost certainly means the program is ineffective; the hub is making calls the spokes can't follow. A conversion rate above 40% at facilities with operative capacity is a meaningful signal that the mechanism is functioning.

What you cannot reliably gate on: how fast the team responds to alerts (this can look good while outcomes are still null, as in INFANT), whether the AI accurately reads the trace (detection accuracy ≠ outcomes), or whether providers are "engaged" with the system.

What should trigger an immediate stop

Four signals should cause immediate pause and external clinical review:

  1. C-section rate rising more than 5 percentage points above the baseline trend without a mortality improvement. Cochrane found that continuous fetal monitoring increases C-sections by about 63% with no mortality benefit. In Bihar, where surgical teams are already stretched, unnecessary C-sections cause real harm to mothers.
  2. Alert volume exceeding 25% of monitored deliveries. Genuine emergencies requiring intervention occur in roughly 5–10% of births. If the hub is flagging a quarter of all deliveries, it's either overcalling or the thresholds have been gamed to inflate conversion rate metrics.
  3. Two or more maternal deaths directly attributable to a hub-recommended emergency action that would not have been taken without the hub's instruction.
  4. More than 20% of emergency transfers result in delivery before reaching the receiving hospital. If this is happening, the transfer pathway is broken and the hub-and-spoke model's most critical fallback doesn't work.

Bet 3 — Intrapartum POCUS (premature)

POCUS stands for point-of-care ultrasound — a handheld ultrasound device used at the bedside. During labor, it can tell you the baby's position and how far down the birth canal it has descended. This matters because conventional clinical examination (manually feeling the baby's position) is wrong 20–70% of the time.

The clinical value of knowing position is real but specific: when the CTG shows distress, knowing whether the baby is face-up (which might respond to repositioning) versus face-down changes whether a C-section is truly needed. Getting that answer faster and more accurately could prevent unnecessary C-sections and enable earlier decisions when they are needed.

Why it's premature to invest now

The problem is the deployment gap. Only ~739 Moyo units have been sold globally since 2015, despite the device costing $198 and being explicitly designed for LMIC facilities. Handheld POCUS has essentially zero intrapartum use in LMICs — it is used almost entirely for antenatal care. No trial has ever combined electronic fetal monitoring with intrapartum POCUS in any LMIC setting.

More fundamentally: the median time from a decision to emergency C-section in eastern Uganda is 110 minutes — and only 2% of cases meet the 30-minute target. Better diagnostic information doesn't fix the theater, the surgeon, or the anesthesiologist.

The right sequencing: deploy SBBC broadly → once Moyo-class monitoring is in widespread use, test whether POCUS decision-support improves outcomes on top → if that feasibility test succeeds, build the integrated platform. The product gap is closable ($3–5M, 2–3 years to prototype). The deployment gap is 5–10x larger and harder.

Janitri / Keyar — the Indian device worth watching

Janitri is a Bangalore-based medtech company that makes the Keyar monitor — a beltless, battery-powered device that captures fetal heart rate and uterine contractions using electrical signals from abdominal patches (rather than the Doppler ultrasound approach used by Moyo). It pairs with a software platform called Daksh that auto-generates the partograph (the standardized labor chart), sends AI-generated distress alerts, and lets a remote doctor supervise multiple labor beds from a single dashboard.

What makes it interesting

  • The task-shifting concept is valuable: one scarce obstetrician supervising many labor beds remotely is exactly the right model for India's health system
  • Real government traction: statewide procurement in Assam, presence in Karnataka, Rajasthan, and Delhi
  • Revenue growing >2x year-over-year, small but real (~$1M FY24–25)
  • More feature-rich than alternatives: the only LMIC device combining contractions, auto-partograph, AI alerts, and remote supervision

The gap that matters

There is no evidence that using Keyar reduces deaths. The validation studies confirm the device captures a signal that agrees with a conventional CTG trace. That is not the same as proving the device prevents birth asphyxia. Crucially, no LMIC intrapartum monitor has outcome RCT evidence — this is a gap for the whole category, not just Janitri. The best proper RCT in the field (Moyo, Tanzania, n=2,973) was null on mortality.

The per-patch consumable cost has never been publicly disclosed. This is the single most important missing number for any cost-effectiveness assessment — it determines whether facilities can sustain use after a grant runs out.

The right role for Janitri

  • India: natural prime partner. It has the government rails, the software, and the regulatory clearance that a foreign device lacks.
  • Sub-Saharan Africa: too early. No CE or WHO-PQ clearance. No distribution network. Laerdal and iCTG (Melody) are ahead on both evidence and relationships. The right play is Janitri's Daksh software paired with an established hardware partner, not Janitri building SSA infrastructure from scratch.

Questions for diligence

Organized by theme. ★ marks true cruxes — things whose answers would change the funding verdict.

On SBBC scale-up

  1. ★ What are the early Nigeria (Borno + Gombe) results showing, and when will they be reportable?

    Nigeria is the first replication of the Tanzania NEJM result in a different health system. If the effect size is similar, it substantially de-risks a large SBBC commitment. If it's smaller or null, we'd want to understand why before committing $30M+.

    For the analyst: ask Laerdal Global Health directly for interim data or site visit reports.

  2. What is the per-facility total cost in Tanzania — and does that hold in Nigeria?

    The PLOS ONE paper reports ~$150k per facility, but that's a specific health system and cost structure. Nigeria, India, and DRC could be meaningfully different. The cost-effectiveness case hinges on this number holding at scale across diverse systems.

    For the analyst: request the SBBC cost model, including recurrent vs. capital split and government co-financing expectations.

  3. ★ How does SBBC work in facilities that don't have emergency C-section capability?

    The bundle's training and resuscitation components help at any facility. But the monitoring component is most valuable when the facility can act on a distress signal surgically. In primary health centers without an operating theater, a Moyo alert may lead to a referral rather than an intervention — and referral time in many settings exceeds safe thresholds. This determines whether SBBC should be targeted at CEmONC-capable facilities only, or whether it has value at a lower level of the health system too.

On AI-CTG / Janitri

  1. ★ What is the consumable cost per delivery for Keyar's adhesive patches — and is there a published number anywhere?

    This is the single most important missing number in the Janitri case. The device hardware is a one-time cost. The patches are recurring — every delivery needs them. If they're $5/delivery at a facility doing 200 deliveries a month, that's $12,000/year just in consumables. If the government doesn't reimburse them, the device idles. We couldn't find a public figure anywhere.

    For the analyst: this needs to come directly from Janitri. Ask for the fully-loaded per-delivery cost including patch, SaaS, data/SIM, and maintenance.

  2. Is there an outcome trial of Keyar planned — and would Janitri accept an independently-run evaluation as a condition of funding?

    The philanthropic case for Keyar funding is strongest if it funds the missing outcome evidence rather than deployment of an unproven device. A stepped-wedge cluster study in high-burden Indian facilities, powered on deaths and asphyxia, would be the right design — and would de-risk not just Janitri but the whole category. Is that something Janitri would support, and who would run it?

  3. What does the Assam NHM deployment actually look like in practice — is the remote dashboard being used?

    Assam is the flagship government deployment: 82 devices across 41 hospitals with a centralized monitoring dashboard. The question is whether the remote supervision model is working — are obstetricians actually watching the dashboard and responding to alerts? Or is this closer to "device installed, software running, not much changing"? A site visit or implementation review would answer this.

  4. ★ If we funded a replication RCT for AI-CTG broadly (not just Keyar), what would the right design be and who would run it?

    The $6–12M AI-CTG replication study is potentially the highest-value-of-information experiment in the whole intrapartum space. It would need: outcomes (deaths, HIE) as primary endpoints; a true LMIC counterfactual comparator (birth attendant without CTG training); separate arms for acquisition, interpretation, and telemonitoring; and sites in diverse health systems. Who are the credible research groups to lead this?

    For the analyst: map the academic groups doing LMIC intrapartum research — Muhimbili (Tanzania), AIIMS (India), KCMC, IHI. Who has done the strongest work and has existing site infrastructure?

On the Gates hub-and-spoke proposal

  1. ★ What is the current Bihar hub-and-spoke conversion rate — of AI alerts, what fraction result in a documented clinical action within 60 minutes?

    This is the single most decision-relevant unmeasured number. Conversion rate <20% confirms the Moyo pattern (detection up, outcomes unchanged) and makes a scale-up case very hard. Conversion rate >40% at operative facilities gives a legitimate basis for Tranche 2 funding. If Gates doesn't have this number, it means the program is being planned without its most critical operational datum.

    For the analyst: ask Piramal/Janitri for the Bihar hub log — how many alerts generated, how many resulted in a documented clinical decision change, within what time window.

  2. ★ Is Gates willing to hold the Tranche 3 ($30M) commitment for 18–24 months pending outcome data — or do they need to commit the full amount upfront?

    The operational gating structure only works if the funder is genuinely willing to withhold the scale-up tranche pending outcomes. If the $50M is effectively pre-committed and the gates are cosmetic, you're back to "fund the RCT." Understanding Gates' actual flexibility on the Tranche 3 gate is necessary before recommending any co-investment structure.

  3. Would Gates accept a stepped-wedge cluster RCT design built into the Bihar rollout from day one?

    The best possible outcome is that the scale-up and the RCT are the same thing. A stepped-wedge design — facilities switching on sequentially in randomised order — costs ~$5–8M on top of the implementation budget and produces publishable causal evidence within 2–3 years. If the result is positive, it unlocks global scale-up with a quality of evidence that monitored implementation never can. If Gates is resistant to this, it's worth understanding why.

    For the analyst: find out who the research partners are on the Bihar deployment. Is Piramal running any evaluation? Is there an academic partner (AIIMS, PHFI)?

On the broader opportunity

  1. What is Gates and Open Philanthropy's current view on SBBC — are they funding scale-up, and what's the gap?

    The GFF $15M innovations challenge is live. Gates has funded Laerdal and Sibel Health (a competing FHR platform). Understanding what's already committed and what the gap is would shape the size and structure of any commitment.

    For the analyst: map the current funder landscape for SBBC — GFF, Gates, CIFF, ELMA — and identify the gap by geography and stage.

  2. Is Sibel Health's ANNE platform (Gates-funded, $17.5M) a competitor or complement to Moyo/SBBC?

    Sibel Health is developing a wireless, skin-patch FHR monitor that could slot into the SBBC framework. If it's a drop-in replacement for Moyo at lower cost or better performance, it could change the SBBC economics. If it's a different deployment model, it's a separate bet. We'd want to understand where it is in development and how Laerdal is thinking about it.

Verdict

The intrapartum space has one clear, investable opportunity and two speculative bets — with a specific funding decision on the Gates Bihar proposal now live.

SBBC scale-up is the only intervention with measured cost-effectiveness and large-scale evidence. The $144/DALY figure is real, the NEJM evidence is strong for its design type, and there is an active deployment pathway through GFF and Laerdal. The key risk is non-replication outside Tanzania — the Nigeria data (2027–28) should be the primary gate for a large commitment. Fund Stage 1 in Nigeria and one other high-burden country now ($5–15M); hold the major scale-up commitment conditional on replication. For context: HBB training alone is $12–23/DALY with RCT-level evidence and addresses the binding constraint (response, not detection). Any intrapartum sensing program needs to beat this bar to justify the technology premium.

The Gates Bihar hub-and-spoke proposal: the hub-and-spoke mechanism is genuinely different from what INFANT tested and worth investigating. But the 250M DALY headline is implausible by 3–50× as a central estimate, and the expected $/DALY is dominated by a ~60–80% probability of null effect. The right structure is a three-tranche commitment: $5M now to build measurement infrastructure, $15M at 12 months gated on conversion rate ≥40% and no overtriage signal, $30M at 18–24 months gated on ≥10% perinatal mortality reduction versus matched controls. Do not commit the $30M tranche on operational metrics alone — that is the INFANT error. If Gates is unwilling to hold the scale-up tranche for 24 months pending outcome data, push for a stepped-wedge RCT design built into the rollout from day one.

AI-CTG replication RCT ($6–12M) is the highest value-of-information experiment available independent of the Gates proposal. A well-designed LMIC cluster RCT with outcomes as the primary endpoint would resolve the core uncertainty for the whole category. VoI: ~$34M in decision value against ~$8M cost (4:1). Worth funding alongside SBBC regardless of what Gates does on Bihar.

Janitri/Keyar is the right India partner for an evidence-generation study. Fund the outcome trial (stepped-wedge, powered on deaths) as a public good for the category — but make the consumable cost disclosure and a functioning response-chain at target sites conditions of funding. Don't fund India deployment without evidence, and don't fund SSA entry until CE/WHO-PQ is secured and a distribution partner is in place.

Intrapartum POCUS is premature. Come back when Moyo-class monitoring has meaningful LMIC penetration and there's evidence on the workflow feasibility question.

The meta-point: Every promising technology in this space runs into the same wall — detecting distress doesn't save babies if the facility can't act. The INFANT trial, Moyo RCTs, and Bihar's 39% anaesthetist coverage all point to the same constraint. The highest-return investment in intrapartum survival is probably not a better monitor — it's improving what happens in the 30 minutes after a distress signal. SBBC is valuable precisely because it attacks that problem directly. Any AI-CTG investment is a bet that the hub-and-spoke mechanism overcomes this constraint. That bet is interesting but unproven, and it takes 24 months of outcome data to evaluate properly.
