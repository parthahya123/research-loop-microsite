US Government Capacity

A Philanthropic Strategy for Democratic Resilience

Thirteen deep research runs — seven on the original framework, six steelmanning and extending it — synthesized into an investment thesis on trust, institutional integrity, crisis response, and the defense of democratic checks.

The question

American trust in the federal government has fallen from 77% in 1964 to roughly 17% today — a 60-point secular collapse with no precedent in the survey record. Across the same period, populist authoritarian movements have grown from fringe to mainstream in most Western democracies, including the United States. The two trends are related: the political science literature finds that declining institutional trust is one of the most robust predictors of populist vote share, and that governments which fail visibly during crises produce lasting populist gains.

The question for a philanthropist is deceptively simple: where should a $50 million per year investment go to arrest this dynamic? Should it go toward fixing the governance failures that populists campaign on — immigration processing, crime, inflation? Toward making government more visible and legible to citizens who don't realize how much it does for them? Toward improving the quality of everyday government interactions like filing taxes or applying for disability benefits? Or toward protecting the institutional infrastructure that might otherwise be dismantled before the next election?

We ran thirteen deep research programs to answer this — seven on the core framework, six attacking its conclusions and extending it into contested territory. What they found is not what most donors expect, and the six steelman runs revised the original synthesis in important ways.

The central finding: trust asymmetry

The best philanthropic dollar prevents a trust point from being destroyed. The second-best preserves the institutional capacity to earn trust in the future. The ordinary dollar — improving services to build trust — barely registers.

The research converges on a single structural finding that reframes every investment decision: trust destruction is fast and large; trust building is slow and tiny. Consider the historical record. Vietnam and Watergate cost 15–20 points of trust each — permanent losses that shaped American politics for decades. Hurricane Katrina destroyed 14 points in months. The 2008 financial crisis subtracted 8–12 points structurally. These were rapid, irreversible events.

What does trust building look like at the same scale? The 9/11 rally was the largest trust-building event in the survey record: +20–25 points. It was gone in nine months. The 1990s economic boom added 12–15 points over six years — and reversed entirely when prosperity ended. A $50M-per-year investment in government service quality improvement produces approximately 0–2 percentage points of aggregate trust gain, within the measurement error of the surveys used to track it.

This asymmetry is not a failure of effort or creativity. It reflects how trust works psychologically: institutions accumulate trust slowly through repeated positive experiences, and lose it rapidly through salient, visible failures. A single spectacular failure — a botched pandemic response, a collapsed bridge, a political scandal — can destroy in weeks what takes a decade to build. The philanthropic implication is stark: the portfolio should be primarily defensive.

But defensive of what, exactly? That question turns out to be more nuanced than it first appears — and the six steelman runs revised the answer.

The trust channel revisited New

The original synthesis concluded that trust in government is not the mediator between government performance and populist voting — that US trust tracks partisan control, that Nordic high-trust countries still produce large populist parties, and that voters barely punish anti-democratic co-partisans. A dedicated steelman run attacked this conclusion as hard as possible. The verdict: the original critique was about 70% right, but wrong in ways that matter for the portfolio.

The trust channel survives — but in a form most donors don't recognize. Di Cocco, Levi, Mariani and Stillman (2024) use three distinct causal identification strategies across European panel data and find that interpersonal trust — trust in other people, not trust in government — causally reduces support for populist parties by 2–6 percentage points. This is genuine causal evidence, not correlation, and it survives three different identification approaches. But the trust construct that survives is not the Pew "trust in government in Washington" question, which is essentially a measure of partisan approval. It is the deeper social trust question: do you think most people can be trusted?

Guriev, Melnikov and Zhuravskaya (2021), using 3G internet rollout as an instrument for information access, find that when citizens gain access to information about government corruption, confidence in government falls and populist parties gain — specifically populist parties, not other opposition parties. The implication is stark: trust decline is often a justified response to revealed misconduct. The fundable intervention is not to "rebuild trust" through communications or service improvements, but to reduce the misconduct that rightfully erodes it.

And Guiso, Herrera, Morelli and Sonno (2024) find that trust in political parties mediates approximately 11% of the total effect of economic insecurity on populist voting. The remaining 82% flows through other channels — direct material grievance, cultural anxiety, identity mobilization. Trust is a channel, not the channel. Its effect size is real but modest.

The key reframe for funders: shift from "competence trust" (which is hard to build and barely moves populist voting) to "integrity trust" (which collapses rapidly when government deceives and is foundational to everything else). The portfolio implication is to redirect resources from service quality messaging toward anti-corruption investments — and to add a new emphasis on social capital building, since interpersonal trust is the construct with the strongest causal evidence.

Integrity capacity New

If deception episodes cause larger, more permanent trust drops than execution failures, then the institutional infrastructure for government honesty — what we call integrity capacity — deserves priority over service quality investments. The evidence supports this strongly.

The US historical pattern is consistent: the major, durable trust drops coincide with credibility crises, not operational failures. Vietnam produced a 24-point trust drop over six years (the "credibility gap" entered American political vocabulary during this period). Watergate cost 17 points. The revelation that the case for Iraq rested on false intelligence drove trust below 30% and it has never recovered. By contrast, execution failures like Hurricane Katrina and the VA wait-time scandal caused bounded, agency-specific drops of 5–10 points that substantially recovered when service improved.

The international comparators are equally instructive. When Turkey's government replaced TurkStat leadership and began publishing manipulated inflation figures, the divergence between official statistics and independent estimates (ENAG) became a proxy for regime credibility. Argentina's INDEC manipulation under the Kirchner government (2007–2015) was so severe that the IMF eventually censured Argentina — the only time in IMF history — and produced lasting damage to Argentine government credibility that persisted even after reform. India's suppression of the 2019 NSSO employment survey, released after the election showing 45-year-high unemployment, damaged statistical credibility precisely through the act of suppression, not just the content. In every case, the damage asymmetry held: deception caused faster, deeper, and less reversible trust erosion than the underlying execution failures would have.

The current US situation, as of October 2026, is historically unusual. The BLS commissioner was fired; statistical agency staff have been cut; 17 inspectors general were removed in January 2025; the EPA Science Advisory Board was dismantled; and the National Weather Service has operated with over 20% vacancy rates at 52 offices after workforce reductions. Each of these represents a degradation of integrity capacity that, based on the international evidence, will impose trust costs that recover slowly if at all.

The philanthropic portfolio for integrity capacity at $3–10M per year has four components. Shadow monitoring — academic teams that replicate BLS, BEA and Census figures from underlying data — creates accountability pressure comparable to what ENAG provided in Turkey. Legal defense for career staff who face retaliation for refusing to alter official statistics or reports addresses the mechanism through which integrity is most directly attacked. Statutory independence advocacy converts informal norms (BLS commissioner independence by tradition) into law (BLS commissioner independence by statute) — the one intervention that creates durable rather than conditional protection. And IG network support fills the oversight gap created by the January 2025 removals.

The research conclusion is direct: integrity capacity should rank above the visible-failures lever in the current threat environment. The case rests on three observations. The damage from deception is 2–5 times larger and far less reversible than the damage from execution failures, so each dollar preventing deception-related trust erosion has a higher expected return. The window for pre-hardening is closing — integrity damage accrues in real time and is partially irreversible, so delay has sharply declining marginal returns. And integrity is the informational substrate on which all other governance improvements depend: if official statistics are not trusted, citizens cannot evaluate whether service quality is improving, and investments in that service quality lose their feedback mechanism.

The 2027–28 failure map New

A separate research run asked: given the policy changes, capacity cuts, and infrastructure decay currently underway, where are the most likely visible government failures in 2027–28, and which ones hit electorally pivotal populations? The answer is alarming in its breadth — seven domains face simultaneous elevated failure risk — and points to a specific reallocation of the existing macro-crisis delivery insurance budget.

The three highest-priority failure domains, ranked by expected electoral damage per preventive dollar:

Medicaid work requirements. Implementation begins in January 2027. CBO scores project 4–5 million coverage losses if requirements are strictly applied; models of implementation stringency vary widely. The affected population — low-income working-age adults, disproportionately rural and non-college — overlaps almost exactly with the populist susceptibility map. Attribution is clear: work requirements are a federal policy choice, and the de Benedictis-Kessner and Warshaw attribution literature predicts federal blame will attach. The critical finding, however, is the interaction effect with rural hospital closures: coverage losses reduce hospital revenue, which accelerates closures, which produces maternal mortality stories in rural swing-state communities. The two domains are one cascade, and addressing Medicaid enrollment navigation simultaneously limits both. Philanthropic role: enrollment navigation in key swing states ($2–3M concentrated in WI, MI, PA, NC rural counties), administrative simplification advocacy, and legal challenges to the most burdensome documentation requirements.

Social Security Administration processing delays. SSA's 7M+ pending claims have grown worse since 2025 staff reductions. Unlike other failure domains, SSA's primary constituency — seniors — is bipartisan, which means failures here attract genuine cross-partisan congressional attention and are less protected by partisan filtering. This is also the failure domain most susceptible to political reversal (both parties need seniors), making early intervention — demonstrating the scale of harm before it fully materializes — more likely to succeed.

Rural hospital closures. 700+ rural hospitals at financial risk; Medicaid reimbursement cuts and the end of COVID stabilization funds are the proximate causes; Medicaid work requirements accelerate the cascade. Rural hospital closures generate the most politically salient sub-narrative in this failure map: communities losing their only OB unit, maternal mortality, and the visible abandonment of rural communities by Washington.

The failure map research also generates an important conditional: if a recession hits in 2027, UI system failures jump to priority one. The COVID-era collapse of legacy COBOL UI systems in New Jersey, New York, California and Pennsylvania — weeks of benefit delays affecting millions of working-class families — is the template for what happens to the 53 state UI systems under surge conditions. The affected population has the highest populist susceptibility of any domain. The portfolio should hold a reserve for rapid redeployment if recession indicators trigger.

Does fixing the governance failure actually work?

The most intuitive theory of change for a philanthropist is: identify the governance failures that populists exploit, fix them, and watch the political fuel drain away. The evidence says otherwise — mostly.

Ten cases across seven democracies where a government made a large, measurable improvement in a governance domain that populists had campaigned on show a consistent pattern: governance fixes reduce issue salience with a 3–6 year lag, but they do not reduce populist vote share. The constituency redistributes to adjacent grievances and rebounds the moment attention slips.

The clearest cases: Denmark reduced asylum applications by 93% between 2019 and 2026. The Danish People's Party collapsed from 21% to 2.6%. But the total far-right vote share held steady at around 15% as voters migrated to newer parties on adjacent cultural issues. Australia stopped boat arrivals almost entirely after 2013 — a genuine policy success — and One Nation grew from 0.2% to 5.4% anyway. The United States in 2024 reduced monthly border encounters by 81% from their peak. Immigration remained the top issue in polling, and Trump won.

The one clean exception is New York City crime in the 1990s. Violent crime fell 57% over a decade; crime as a top issue fell from 52% to under 10%; no durable populist movement emerged. The difference is diagnostic: crime is a material grievance. When you stop being mugged, you feel safer. Immigration is substantially a cultural and identity grievance. Even if border crossings reach zero, the existing immigrant population, demographic change, and cultural anxiety persist. The governance metric is a symbol of the underlying anxiety, not the anxiety itself.

Immigration: the harder look New

A steelman run investigated whether the "immigration bet is dead" conclusion was too strong. It was — in one specific dimension.

The original conclusion conflated four meaningfully different immigration governance levers: asylum adjudication speed, border enforcement, legal immigration processing, and interior enforcement. The evidence against the bet is primarily evidence about border enforcement and restrictive rhetoric — the Australia and Denmark cases — not about adjudication speed or system competence. These are not the same thing.

One specific bet survives: asylum adjudication speed. The US backlog sits at over 3 million pending cases, with resolution times of 5+ years. Faster adjudication — reducing case resolution to under one year — works through a different mechanism than border enforcement. It shrinks the limbo population that generates visible disorder, produces more enforceable outcomes (both grants and removals), undermines the "broken system" media frame, and over 5–7 years converts recent-arrival populations to established populations with the contact dynamics that reduce backlash. None of this depends on crossing-level enforcement.

The steelman run also identified a variable the original synthesis missed: rhetorical ownership. The evidence consistently shows that governance achievement without political credit-claiming fails to reduce salience. The Biden 2024 case — an 81% reduction in border encounters, delivered without rhetorical ownership — sits in the worst possible cell: you incur the political costs of engagement without reaping any credit. Denmark's success was not just the 93% asylum reduction; it was that the Social Democrats ran on it aggressively as their signature achievement. Clinton's 1996 immigration achievement worked similarly — genuine enforcement plus aggressive rhetorical ownership. The lesson from the Biden failure is not "achievement doesn't matter." It is "achievement without ownership doesn't matter."

Philanthropy can fund the governance improvement (adjudication capacity, legal representation, process reform) but cannot control whether a political party claims credit for it. This is a real limitation that conditions the investment. But the adjudication speed bet is sufficiently different from the border enforcement bet that it deserves a separate evaluation — and on that evaluation, it narrowly survives.

Economic shocks in a two-party system New

Most evidence that economic shocks raise populist vote share comes from multi-party European systems where a new far-right party absorbs the swing. In the United States, the same shock shows up differently: as candidate radicalization within primaries, not as third-party vote share gains. This distinction matters enormously for what philanthropy should be trying to move.

The Autor, Dorn and Hanson China shock research finds that trade-exposed congressional districts shifted politically — but the effect in the US runs through candidate ideology in primaries, not through general election vote switching between parties. Andrew Hall's work shows that extreme primary winners systematically underperform in general elections, but that primary electorates keep selecting them in economically distressed districts anyway. The financial crisis, the China shock, and the 2021–24 inflation all radicalized candidate selection inside the Republican Party without producing equivalent third-party surges. The Tea Party, the Freedom Caucus, and the MAGA primary wave were all within-party phenomena.

This reframes what the existing macro-crisis delivery insurance tier is actually protecting against. UI modernization, SNAP delivery reliability, and Medicaid continuity are not preventing a populist third-party surge. They are potentially preventing the kind of household economic distress that creates anti-system sentiment that gets channeled into primary radicalization. The causal chain is: delivery failure → extended economic distress → anti-system sentiment → extreme candidates win primaries → more authoritarian governance. The COVID UI collapse in 2020 — weeks of benefit delays in NJ, NY, CA, and PA — is the most direct natural experiment available for testing whether this chain holds at the primary level, and that study has not been done.

The single highest-value-of-information investment identified by this research run is a $500K–1M academic study: does UI delivery failure during COVID predict more extreme candidates winning 2022 Republican primaries in affected districts, controlling for pre-existing ideology? If yes, the shock insurance case is validated. If no, the resources should shift toward primary participation and candidate recruitment. Commission it.

Crisis response as trust insurance

COVID-19 is the cleanest natural experiment available in the trust literature. The same pathogen hit every country in the same year; governments varied dramatically in response quality; and we can observe both trust trajectories and subsequent populist vote outcomes. The causal chain holds across the full dataset.

In countries with top-quartile responses — Taiwan, South Korea, Norway, New Zealand — trust rose 8 to 15 points, and far-right vote share fell 2 to 4 points at the next election. In bottom-quartile countries — the United States, United Kingdom, Brazil — trust fell 6 to 14 points and far-right vote share rose 4 to 12 points. The Cambridge Centre for the Future of Democracy documented a global "flight to competence" in 2020: initial populist collapse wherever governments delivered, and a rebound exceeding pre-COVID levels wherever they bungled it. The trust-to-populist-vote elasticity across the dataset runs approximately +1 trust point to −0.4 points of populist vote share.

This is the strongest causal finding across all research runs. And it points to a specific philanthropic opportunity: government will fund crisis response during crises. It reliably cuts those budgets 3–5 years afterward. Every pandemic preparedness budget in living memory has been slashed within a half-decade of the last pandemic. Philanthropy's comparative advantage is sustaining investment through the dry periods between crises, and advocating for preparedness budgets when urgency has faded. Estimated leverage on preparedness advocacy: $5–20 million per year protects $5–10 billion per year in government capacity spending at roughly 500:1.

The key uncertainty here, flagged in the original synthesis, is partisan filtering. Hurricane Helene's 22-point partisan gap in response quality evaluation is the most concerning signal: if partisans now judge government response quality primarily on party affiliation rather than actual performance, the trust payoff from crisis preparedness is capped at around 5 points rather than the historical 15-point maximum. If this crux resolves badly, shift weight from crisis preparedness toward institutional hardening.

How fast does populism degrade capacity?

Five cases of democratic backsliding — Hungary from 2010, Poland under PiS from 2015, Turkey post-2016, India from 2014, and the United States from 2025 — show a convergent playbook: capture courts first, purge personnel second, hollow statistical agencies third, squeeze independent media throughout. The US case shows the fastest Year 1 degradation velocity ever documented on the V-Dem liberal democracy index.

The decisive finding: norms-based protections failed in every single case without exception. Independent media cultures, professional civil service conventions, unwritten norms of restraint — all were overridden when a determined government chose to act. Only structural protections held: constitutional provisions with enforcement mechanisms, statutory requirements backed by independent courts, and decentralized authority that no single actor could override. Poland's constitutional tribunal was restored under Tusk in 2023 because it had statutory backing and institutional memory persisted in the career civil service. Hungary's equivalent institutions did not survive because they had no structural anchor.

This finding has a direct implication: the philanthropic strategy must convert norms into statutes. Government cannot fund its own defense against its own leadership. The private sector has no return on investment here. No other actor is positioned to fill this gap.

Elite defection New

The only US checks that held against executive overreach in 2025–26 were a Supreme Court majority, one senator's hold on a Fed nominee, state attorneys general, and pro bono litigators. A dedicated research run investigated what makes co-partisan elites defect from an authoritarian-leaning leader and whether this rate is philanthropically movable.

The comparative literature on elite defection — Hungary, Poland, Venezuela, Turkey, Brazil 2022, the US 2020–21 — reveals that defection is rare, difficult, and dependent on a specific conjunction of conditions. The most striking finding is not that defection sometimes succeeds but that it almost always fails or never materializes. The primary constraint in every case is the fear of being primaried from the right. In Hungary, complete capture occurred because no Fidesz elite defected at the critical early juncture. In Poland, defection and recovery succeeded because statutory protections created a scaffold for the returning opposition. The Brazil 2022 case, where business elites and military leadership backed away from supporting the January 8 events, offers the most hopeful lesson: cascade defection can happen suddenly once a credible insider challenger emerges.

The honest assessment: elite defection is marginally movable by philanthropy at $5–10M per year, not fundamentally movable. The primary threat — fear of being primaried from the right — is sustained by the organic loyalty of Republican primary electorates, which is a function of political identity, media ecosystem, and cultural polarization that $5–10M cannot transform.

What philanthropy can do at this scale, specifically:

  • Legal defense fund ($1.5M/yr c3): Provide legal defense for career officials who refuse illegal orders, whistleblowers, and lawyers targeted by executive retaliation. Reduces the personal cost of defection at the margin. The chilling effect on pro bono legal challenges is real and documented; addressing it has a clear theory of change.
  • Career transition fellowships ($1M/yr c3): 20 fellowships for career civil servants who leave government rather than comply with illegal or unethical orders. Signals that defection has a soft landing.
  • Business coalition coordination ($1M/yr c4): A standing coalition of businesses committed to specific institutional norms (Fed independence, IG independence, rule of law) that engages on specific legislative votes. Business pressure has mattered episodically — the Tillis Fed hold, Senate tariff pushback — but dissipates without coordination infrastructure.
  • Structural reform: ranked-choice voting and open primaries ($500K/yr c3): The most promising long-run solution to the primary threat constraint. At $500K, philanthropy is a co-investor in state-level campaigns with a 5–10 year payoff horizon.

The most realistic framing is not a strategy to dramatically increase elite defection, but a program to maintain institutional resilience and create conditions for defection at the margins. When defection does come — and the Hungary case shows it can transform the political landscape suddenly — it requires pre-existing infrastructure that philanthropy can build and maintain during the long period of apparent futility.

The investment portfolio

Incorporating the six new research runs, the portfolio shifts in three ways from the original synthesis: integrity capacity is elevated to Tier 1, the failure map sharpens the macro-crisis delivery allocation, and elite defection is added as a new line item.

Tier 1 — Defensive core ($35M/yr)

Integrity capacity and institutional hardening ($18M/yr). This is now the highest-priority tier, elevated from its original position. It funds: shadow statistical monitoring and BLS/BEA data preservation ($3M), legal defense for career civil servants and IG advocacy ($4M), state AG litigation coordination ($4M), statutory independence legislation advocacy ($3M), FOIA enforcement and transparency litigation ($2M), and CNSTAT/NAS scientific advisory reconstruction ($2M). Organizations: POGO, CREW, Protect Democracy, CNSTAT.

Crisis response capacity and preparedness ($10M/yr). The strongest causal finding in the portfolio — COVID natural experiment, 500:1 preparedness leverage. Funds crisis communication and counter-disinformation R&D ($3M), preparedness advocacy through dry periods ($3M), emergency management training and community resilience ($2M), and after-action research infrastructure ($2M).

Elite defection infrastructure ($7M/yr, c3+c4). Legal defense fund for officials refusing illegal orders ($1.5M c3), career transition fellowships ($1M c3), business coalition coordination ($1M c4), communications infrastructure for defectors ($1M c4), electoral support for moderate candidates in viable down-ballot races ($1.5M c4), RCV/open primaries co-investment ($500K c3), and democratic resilience research ($500K c3).

Tier 2 — Failure prevention ($10M/yr)

Macro-crisis delivery and failure map prevention ($10M/yr). Medicaid enrollment navigation concentrated in swing-state rural counties — this single investment addresses both the Medicaid coverage loss failure and the rural hospital closure cascade ($3M). SSA backlog advocacy and bipartisan restoration effort ($2M). UI system modernization technical assistance for the 10 most vulnerable state COBOL systems, with $500K held in reserve for rapid redeployment if recession indicators trigger ($2M + $500K reserve). Rural hospital financial advocacy ($1.5M). Safety net adequacy advocacy ($1M).

Tier 3 — Welfare-building complement ($5M/yr)

High-impact touchpoint improvement ($5M/yr). IRS/EITC access expansion — VITA network, Direct File support for 25 million EITC families ($2M). SSA disability navigation ($1.5M). Admin burden reduction research and advocacy ($1M). Digital service innovation ($500K). Justified primarily on direct welfare grounds for affected populations; the trust-to-populism case is secondary and uncertain.

Reserve — Measurement and opportunistic response ($5M/yr)

Trust measurement infrastructure — partisan-decomposed series tracking non-partisan residual ($1.5M). The $500K UI study commission (high-value-of-information investment on whether shock insurance prevents primary radicalization). Portfolio evaluation and attribution ($1M). Strategic reserve for crisis-moment redeployment ($2M).

What the research says not to fund

Fixing the governance failure to defuse populist grievances

The salience evidence across ten cases in seven democracies is discouraging: governance improvements reduce issue salience with a 3–6 year lag but do not reduce populist vote share. The constituency redistributes to adjacent issues. The only exception — NYC crime in the 1990s — required a material rather than identity grievance, a 50%+ multi-decade improvement, bipartisan rhetorical ownership, and no adjacent pivot for the populist. None of those conditions apply to most contemporary populist grievances.

The partial exception is asylum adjudication speed, which survives scrutiny as a bounded bet (see the immigration section). The general principle that "fix the governance failure → populist fuel drains" does not survive.

Making government legible to build trust

Mettler's submerged state finding is real — 40–60% of benefit recipients don't recognize their benefits as governmental. But making government visible doesn't build trust. The meta-analytic correlation between transparency interventions and trust is r = +0.13. The HMRC federal-scale test found no trust gain and a possible backfire. Five documented backfire mechanisms: learning about spending activates anti-spending attitudes; costs trigger "waste" narratives; visibility without quality improvement highlights failures; partisan framing polarizes; "government helps you" messaging activates identity-protective cognition among self-reliance voters. Administrative burden reduction works; information campaigns don't.

Improving services broadly to build aggregate trust

Gate 1 shows service improvement generates ~0–2pp aggregate trust from $50M/yr — within measurement error. The specific-to-diffuse support gap is the binding constraint: improvements move satisfaction with the specific agency but weakly translate to trust in "the government." IRS Direct File achieved NPS +80 among users; aggregate IRS trust did not measurably move.

More fundamentally: honesty moves trust 2–4× more than competence. The public responds to perceived integrity, not perceived capability. This means competence investments should be justified on welfare grounds, not aggregate trust grounds.

E-government and digital transformation as anti-populism strategy

Estonia's e-government model required building from scratch in a population of under 1.5 million with a post-Soviet baseline and two decades of sustained investment. The implied cost at federal agency scale is $500M–2B per trust point over 3–7 years. US digital service reform (USDS, 18F) demonstrates genuine quality improvements at specific touchpoints, but the binding constraint on US government service quality is institutional capacity and political will, not technology — and technology investments face the specific-to-diffuse problem just as severely as other service improvements.

The cruxes — what would change the verdict

There are seven factual questions that, if they resolved differently, would significantly change the portfolio.

Has partisan filtering now captured most crisis evaluation? Hurricane Helene showed a 22-point partisan gap in response quality assessment. If partisan filtering dominates crisis evaluation for more than half the public, the crisis response trust payoff is capped at ~5pp rather than the historical 15pp maximum. If so, shift weight from crisis preparedness toward integrity capacity and elite defection infrastructure. This is the most decision-relevant crux.

Does UI delivery failure predict primary radicalization? The $500K academic study should resolve this. If yes, the shock insurance budget is fully justified on anti-radicalization grounds. If no, redirect those resources toward primary participation.

Other cruxes, in brief:

  • Trust → populism causal chain post-2020: If the COVID experiment and FST dataset hold, the full portfolio is justified. If extreme polarization has broken the chain, only integrity capacity and elite defection survive as standalone investments.
  • US institutional degradation reversibility: Poland reversed under Tusk because statutory protections preserved institutional memory. Hungary did not. The US outcome depends substantially on 2028+. Under non-reversal, the hardening budget needs 3–5× the allocation.
  • Asylum adjudication speed and rhetorical ownership: The immigration bet survives only if (a) the adjudication mechanism works through the proposed pathway and (b) a future administration is willing to claim credit for a functional system. If Democratic politicians are structurally unable to campaign on immigration governance improvements, the investment has no political payoff.
  • A major crisis within five years: Likely, given elevated risk levels. If it arrives, crisis response and macro-delivery investments pay off immediately. If not, they remain latent insurance.
  • AI disinformation and crisis communication: 2024 showed less AI dominance than feared, but capabilities are growing. If AI disinformation overwhelms crisis communication infrastructure, pivot toward platform regulation advocacy.

  • Financial Crisis — Does the FST result generalize to 2020–24 inflation?

  • Reverse Arrow — How fast does populism degrade capacity? Can we harden the US before 2029?

  • US Failure Map — Where are the next visible failures in 2027–28? Medicaid, SSA, rural hospitals, UI

  • Shocks in a Two-Party System — Primary radicalization as the right outcome variable; the UI study commission
