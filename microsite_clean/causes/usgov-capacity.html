US Government Capacity

A Philanthropic Strategy for Democratic Resilience

Seven deep research runs on trust, salience, crisis response, institutional degradation, and economic shocks — synthesized into a $50M/yr investment thesis.

Central Thesis

Defend capacity; don't try to build trust. The best philanthropic dollar prevents a trust point from being destroyed. The second-best dollar preserves the institutional capacity to earn trust in the future. The ordinary dollar — improving services to build trust — barely registers.

This is counterintuitive. Donors want to build things. But the data is unambiguous: trust destruction is fast and large; trust building is slow and tiny. The strategy flows from that asymmetry, not from intuition about what "better government" should look like.

The trust asymmetry

American trust in the federal government has fallen from 77% (1964) to 17% (2025) — a 60-point secular collapse. Seven research runs investigated every plausible mechanism for reversing it. They converge on a single structural finding: the speed and scale of trust destruction vastly exceeds the speed and scale of trust building. This reframes everything.

Trust Destruction — Fast & Large
Vietnam War (1964–72)−15 to −20pp, permanent
Watergate (1973–74)−12 to −15pp, partially permanent
Hurricane Katrina (2005)−14pp, months

Afghanistan withdrawal (2021)−6 to −8pp
Trust Building — Slow & Small
9/11 rally (2001)+20–25pp, gone in 9 months

IRS Direct File pilot (2024)+0pp national trust

The implication is structural: you cannot build your way out of a trust deficit at philanthropic scale. The largest trust-building events in the 68-year record are either macro-economic prosperity (not a philanthropic deliverable) or crisis rallies that dissipate in months. What philanthropy CAN do is reduce the frequency and depth of trust-destroying events — by maintaining crisis response capacity, hardening institutions against degradation, and improving the handful of government touchpoints where bad service concentrates among the most distrustful Americans.

The key number: A $50M/year investment in government service quality improvement produces approximately 0–2 percentage points of aggregate trust gain — within the measurement error of the surveys used to track trust. For comparison, a botched hurricane response destroys 14 points in months. This is why the portfolio is defensive.

Seven gate questions

Each research run answered a distinct causal gate. An investment approach that fails any gate is eliminated or downgraded. The gates are cumulative — the portfolio is what survives all seven.

Gate 1 — Trust Residual
Does performance move the non-partisan component of trust at meaningful scale?
Barely. ~0–2pp from $50M/yr. Honesty moves trust 2–4× more than competence.
Gate 2 — Salience Fix
Does fixing a governance failure lower populist salience and vote share?
Lowers salience (3–6yr lag) but not populist vote share. Cultural grievances are immune. The immigration bet is dead in its strong form.
Gate 3 — Submerged State
Does making government legible and visible change trust?
No. Meta-analytic r = +0.13. HMRC federal-scale test: zero trust gain, possible backfire. Burden reduction works; information campaigns don't.
Gate 4 — Crisis Response
Does crisis response quality matter for trust and subsequent populist vote?
YES — strongest finding. COVID natural experiment validates the full causal chain. +1pp trust ≈ −0.4pp populist vote. 500:1 leverage on preparedness advocacy.
Gate 5 — Financial Crisis
Does macro (inflation, financial crises) dominate so much that micro interventions are noise?
Macro dominates the level, but philanthropy acts at the delivery layer. UI modernization is the fundable lever. Can't prevent the shock; can blunt the trust-destroying failure of delivery.
Gate 6 — Touchpoints
Which government interactions reach the most distrust-heavy populations?
IRS/EITC (145M filers, ACSI dead last, 25M working-class families) and SSA disability (7M+ applicants, 7mo average wait). Medicaid excluded: attribution problem kills the trust channel.
Gate 7 — Reverse Arrow
How fast does populist government degrade capacity? Is pre-hardening fundable?
Fastest Year 1 degradation ever documented in the US. Norms fail; statutes hold. Only philanthropy can fund government's defense against its own leadership.

Does fixing the governance failure actually work?

The intuitive theory of change — fix the broken thing populists campaign on, and they lose their fuel — fails in its strong form across every immigration-adjacent case. Ten cases across seven democracies since 1990 show a consistent pattern: governance competence reduces issue salience with a 3–6 year lag, but does not reduce populist vote share. The constituency redistributes to adjacent issues, then rebounds the moment accommodation weakens.

NYC crime is the only case of complete defusion. The difference: crime is a material grievance — when you stop being mugged, you feel safer. Immigration is substantially a cultural/identity grievance. Even if crossings go to zero, the existing immigrant population, demographic change, and cultural anxiety persist. The governance metric is a symbol of the underlying anxiety, not the anxiety itself. Fixing the symbol cannot cure the condition.

The Krause/Cohen/Abou-Chadi Finding
Mainstream parties adopting tough immigration rhetoric legitimizes rather than deflates far-right support. Their study answers "does saying tough things work?" (no), not "does doing tough things work?" — but both answers are now negative. The US 2024 case — the strongest test of quiet competence — confirms: a massive, measurable improvement delivered without rhetorical ownership failed to move the needle within an electoral cycle. The minimum lag for any effect is 3–6 years. The political window is typically 9–18 months. These are structurally incompatible.

Crisis response as trust insurance

COVID is the cleanest natural experiment available in the trust literature: the same pathogen hit every country, governments differed dramatically in response quality, and we can observe both trust and populist vote outcomes. The causal chain holds across the full dataset.

Country / Response tierTrust change 2019→2020Populist vote change (next election)
Top-quartile (Taiwan, S. Korea, Norway, NZ)+8 to +15ppFar-right −2 to −4pp
Mid-quartile (Germany, Australia, Canada)Stable to +5ppFar-right stable to −2pp
Bottom-quartile (US, UK, Brazil)−6 to −14ppFar-right +4 to +12pp

The Cambridge Centre for the Future of Democracy documented a global "flight to competence" during COVID — populist support collapsed initially in most countries. Where governments delivered, that flight persisted. Where they bungled it, populism rebounded and exceeded pre-COVID levels. The trust→populist vote elasticity across the dataset: +1 trust point ≈ −0.4pp populist vote share (range −0.2 to −0.6pp). Ten preserved trust points could be worth 4pp of populist vote — potentially the difference between a functional democracy and an authoritarian turn in marginal cases.

The philanthropic role in crisis preparedness
Government will fund crisis response during crises. It reliably cuts those budgets 3–5 years later — the between-crisis budget-cut cycle is the single biggest structural threat to response capacity. Every pandemic preparedness budget is slashed within a half-decade of the last pandemic. Philanthropy's comparative advantage is sustaining investment through dry periods. An estimated $5–20M/yr in preparedness advocacy protects $5–10B/yr in government capacity spending at ~500:1 leverage — the cheapest trust points in the entire portfolio.

The reverse causal arrow

A five-country comparison (Hungary, Poland 2015–23, Turkey, India, US 2025) reveals a convergent playbook: capture courts first, purge personnel second, hollow statistics third, squeeze independent media throughout. Speed varies, but the sequence is remarkably stable — and the US 2025 trajectory shows the fastest Year 1 degradation velocity ever documented in the comparative literature.

CaseFirst targetSpeed to major thresholdWhat proved resilientReversible?
Hungary (2010–) Constitutional Court ~18 months to Partly Free Nothing — complete capture No
Poland PiS (2015–23) Constitutional Tribunal ~24 months Cities, private media, civil society Partial (Tusk 2023)
Turkey (2014–) Judiciary ~6 months post-2016 Bond market constraints No
India (2014–) Statistical agencies Slow degradation; ongoing Federalism, Supreme Court Partial
US (2025–) Agency leadership + Schedule F Fastest Year 1 ever on V-Dem Federal courts (so far); federalism Uncertain — depends on 2028+

The decisive finding: norms-based protections failed in every single case without exception. Independent media culture, professional civil service conventions, unwritten restraint — all were overridden once a determined government chose to do so. Only structural protections held: constitutional provisions, statutory requirements, decentralized authority no single actor can override. Poland's constitutional tribunal survived to be restored because it had statutory backing. Hungary's independent institutions did not.

This means the philanthropic strategy must convert norms into statutes. Government cannot fund its own defense against its own leadership. The private sector has no ROI. No other actor is positioned to fill this gap.

What doesn't work

"Fix the governance failure to defuse populist grievances"

The salience evidence shows governance fixes reduce issue salience with a 3–6 year lag but do NOT reduce populist vote share. The cultural-identity layer of most populist grievances is immune to administrative improvement. The only clean defusion case (NYC crime, 1990s) required conditions that are essentially unreplicable for immigration: a material rather than cultural grievance, a dramatic 50%+ improvement, bipartisan rhetorical ownership, sustained decade+, and no adjacent pivots for the populist to exploit.

The immigration bet specifically: quiet competence has never defused immigration salience in any democracy. The US 2024 case — the most direct test — confirms that an 81% reduction in border encounters, delivered without rhetorical ownership, failed to move salience within the electoral window. The minimum effective lag (3–6 years) is structurally incompatible with the political cycle.

"Tell people about government programs to build trust"

Mettler's submerged state finding is real: 40–60% of benefit recipients don't recognize their benefits as governmental. The SNAP card looks like a debit card; the mortgage interest deduction is invisible; ACA plans come from private insurers. But making government visible doesn't build trust. The meta-analytic correlation between transparency and trust is r = +0.13 — real but tiny. The largest federal-scale legibility test (HMRC tax breakdown letters, 2012–14) found no trust gain and a possible backfire effect.

Five documented backfire mechanisms: (1) learning about spending activates anti-spending attitudes; (2) learning about program costs triggers "waste" narratives; (3) visibility without quality improvement highlights failures; (4) partisan framing of visible programs polarizes rather than unifies; (5) "government helps you" messaging activates identity-protective cognition among self-reliance voters. The one lever that works within this domain is administrative burden reduction — which is captured in the touchpoints approach.

"Improve government services broadly to build aggregate trust"

Gate 1 shows service improvement generates ~0–2pp aggregate trust from $50M/yr — within measurement error. The specific-vs-diffuse support gap is the binding constraint: improvements move satisfaction with the specific agency (VA trust rose from 47% to 80% over a decade) but weakly translate to trust in "the government." IRS Direct File achieved an NPS of +80 among users; aggregate trust in the IRS did not measurably move. Gate 6 shows depth at a few high-impact touchpoints outperforms breadth across many agencies.

More fundamentally: honesty and probity move trust 2–4× more than competence and efficiency. The public responds to perceived integrity, not perceived capability. Citizens care more about "are they lying to me?" than "are they running things efficiently?" This means competence investments should be justified on welfare grounds for affected populations, not on aggregate trust grounds.

"Digital government / e-government will transform the trust picture"

Estonia's e-government model is the canonical success story, but it required: building from scratch, a small (<1.5M people) homogeneous high-trust society, a post-Soviet context where the baseline was zero rather than legacy COBOL systems, and two decades of sustained investment. The implied cost to replicate this at federal agency scale in the US is $500M–2B per trust point with 3–7 year time-to-impact.

US digital service reform (USDS, 18F) has demonstrated genuine quality improvements at specific touchpoints — Healthcare.gov recovery, HealthCare.gov improvements, VA online services. These improvements are worthwhile on welfare grounds. But the binding constraint on US government service quality is institutional capacity and political will, not technology — and technology investments face the specific-vs-diffuse problem just as severely as other service improvements.

The most decision-relevant crux is the second: if partisan filtering now dominates crisis evaluation (>50% of the public judges government response quality on partisan lines regardless of actual performance), then the crisis response trust payoff is capped at ~5pp rather than the 15pp historical maximum. Hurricane Helene's 22-point partisan gap in response evaluation is the most concerning signal. If this crux resolves badly, shift weight from Tier 1B (crisis preparedness, which works less) toward Tier 1A (institutional hardening, which works regardless of the trust channel).
