Noora Health — NCD Program

Caregiver education for hypertension and diabetes: what the evidence actually shows

A synthesis of ten research runs covering adherence durability, DALY value, the hypertension cascade, counterfactual government care, benchmarks, evidence validity, and the Indonesia opportunity.

Research runs completed October 20, 2026 · 10 topics · Sources: PubMed, GBD 2021, NFHS-5, Riskesdas 2018, BDHS, Noora annual reports, IDinsight, WHO-CHOICE

The short version

Noora Health trains family caregivers of hospitalized patients to support medication adherence and chronic disease management after discharge. The program is cheap — roughly $1 per patient educated — scalable, and embedded in government hospital infrastructure across India and Bangladesh. It addresses a genuine gap: in all three target countries, structured adherence counselling for diagnosed NCD patients is essentially absent from routine government care.

The cost-effectiveness case is more complicated than the program's simplicity suggests. Three tensions define the analysis.

First, the adherence gain from a single brief education session probably decays within 3–6 months in the absence of boosters, based on the closest analogues in the literature. The caregiver-mediation model Noora uses — training a family member rather than the patient directly — is theoretically more durable but remains essentially unstudied at 12-month follow-up in an LMIC setting.

Second, the hypertension cascade in India means that only about 5% of hypertension-attributable CVD DALYs are reachable by an adherence-only intervention. The vast majority of the burden sits upstream — in the 72% of Indian hypertensives who are unaware they have the condition at all. Bangladesh and Indonesia have more favorable cascade shapes for adherence work.

Third, Noora's evidence base for the NCD program is thin. The headline result — 70% versus 52% MARS-measured adherence for hypertension — is unpublished, uses an instrument never validated in India for NCD populations, and almost certainly overstates the objective adherence gap by 17–25 percentage points. The direction of effect is probably real. The magnitude is not credible as reported.

The strongest affirmative case for Noora's NCD program is not the general hypertension/diabetes adherence channel at all. It is secondary prevention — post-stroke, post-MI, post-cardiac-surgery patients, who generate 10–13 times more DALYs per incremental adherent patient-year than newly diagnosed primary-care hypertensives, and whom Noora's hospital-based model reaches naturally.

What Noora's NCD program actually consists of

The NCD Care Companion Program is an extension of the same Care Companion model Noora developed for maternal and newborn care and cardiac surgery. The delivery architecture is identical; only the content changes.

Noora partners with state governments to embed the program in public health facilities. It trains 3–4 Master Trainers per facility — existing government healthcare staff, typically nurses or counsellors, not Noora employees — who then deliver condition-specific group sessions to patients and their family members. Sessions happen in hospital wards, hallways, or waiting areas during the patient's admission, typically in the 30–45 minutes before discharge. Groups of 5–30 participants watch locally-produced videos and go through illustrated flipcharts covering medication adherence, vital sign monitoring, diet, physical activity, warning symptoms, and when to return to care.

Families can opt into a WhatsApp-based follow-up service ("Aarogya Seva") that sends reminders and allows queries to practitioners. In Punjab specifically, a dedicated Blood Pressure Care Companion WhatsApp line sends appointment reminders and corrects common misconceptions. There is also a documented 14-week pilot using automated IVR calls for daily reminders, though this is not standard across all sites.

What the program does not currently do: it does not screen accompanying family members for undiagnosed hypertension or diabetes (that is the caregiver's role, not the purpose of the session). It does not provide ongoing follow-up visits. It does not start or adjust medications. It does not check BP or HbA1c at follow-up as part of the program protocol.

As of late 2025, the NCD program operates primarily in Punjab (since 2022, through Health and Wellness Centres in Moga and Ferozpur districts), with a hospital-based component in Andhra Pradesh and Karnataka alongside Noora's broader inpatient program. Bangladesh NCD launched more recently, with an evaluation underway but results not yet published. No Indonesia NCD program exists yet.

Where the hypertension burden actually sits

Before evaluating any adherence intervention, it is worth understanding what fraction of the total NCD burden that intervention can actually touch. An adherence-only program reaches only patients who are already diagnosed and already on treatment but not yet controlled — the "treated-uncontrolled" category of the cascade.

The hypertension cascade in India is sobering. Among approximately 220 million hypertensive adults, only 28% are aware of their condition, roughly half of those (52%) are on medication, and about 60% of those treated achieve blood pressure control. The net result is that 9% of Indian hypertensives have controlled BP. The cascade decomposes as: 72% unaware, 13% aware-but-untreated, 6% treated-but-uncontrolled, 9% controlled. The treated-uncontrolled category — the only one an adherence intervention directly reaches — contains roughly 6% of hypertensives and accounts for an estimated 5% of hypertension-attributable CVD DALYs in India.

Bangladesh presents a strikingly different picture. Awareness has reached 54% of hypertensives — double India's rate — and treatment stands at 45% of all hypertensives. The cascade therefore has more people in the treated-uncontrolled stage: an estimated 26% of hypertension DALYs may be reachable by adherence interventions. Bangladesh's cascade shape is substantially more favorable for Noora-style work.

Indonesia sits in between. Awareness is only about 35% — similar to India — but treatment rates among those who are aware are much higher (83%). The result is a distinctive profile: a large unaware majority, but among those in the system, a substantial treated-uncontrolled population. Around 16% of hypertension DALYs in Indonesia may be accessible to adherence interventions, concentrated in patients who are nominally in care but not achieving control. This "treated-uncontrolled bulge" is a particularly meaningful target because it suggests a system failure at the adherence and intensification stages rather than at diagnosis — exactly what caregiver education can address.

The diabetes cascade follows a similar pattern across all three countries, with generally lower awareness but a similar structure of losses at each stage.

The implication for program design is significant. In India, investing in case-finding (reaching the 72% unaware) would unlock far more DALYs than investing in adherence support for the existing treated population. In Bangladesh and Indonesia, adherence-focused work is more efficient because more of the burden is already in the treated-uncontrolled stage.

The DALY math: what an adherent patient-year is worth

The central calculation for any adherence intervention is: how many DALYs does an incremental adherent patient-year avert? This is the denominator against which program cost is divided to get $/DALY.

The calculation chain for hypertension runs: improved adherence → lower SBP → fewer strokes and IHD events → DALYs averted. The best available RCT evidence from the Blood Pressure Lowering Treatment Trialists' Collaboration (ESC 2026, pooling 91,339 participants across 9 RCTs) finds that the treatment effect in PDC ≥80% patients is 5.2 mmHg SBP reduction versus 3.0 mmHg in PDC <80% patients. The real-world causal SBP difference between adherent and non-adherent patients is approximately 5 mmHg (range 3–7 mmHg), after adjusting conservatively for healthy-adherer bias that inflates observational estimates. Using the Lewington 2002 meta-analysis (each 2 mmHg SBP = 7% relative stroke risk reduction, 5% IHD risk reduction) and GBD 2021 India stroke and IHD DALY rates for ages 45–65, the central estimate is approximately 0.02 DALYs per incremental adherent hypertension patient-year (range 0.01–0.04).

For type 2 diabetes on oral agents, the channel is wider: adherence affects both microvascular complications (retinopathy, nephropathy, neuropathy) and macrovascular events through HbA1c reduction. A fully adherent patient achieves roughly 1.0–1.5% lower HbA1c than a non-adherent counterpart. Using the UKPDS risk engine and GBD 2021 diabetes DALY rates, the central estimate is approximately 0.05 DALYs per incremental adherent diabetes patient-year (range 0.03–0.08). Diabetes generates roughly 2.5 times more DALYs per adherent patient-year than hypertension, primarily because microvascular complications have no hypertension equivalent at this margin.

These numbers set tight cost constraints. At a $50/DALY threshold, the maximum defensible program cost per additional adherent patient-year generated is $1.00 (hypertension) or $2.50 (diabetes). At $125/DALY, the ceiling rises to $2.50 and $6.25. For a $1 per patient educational session to be cost-effective, it must move approximately one in two patients to durable adherence — a very demanding standard — or the effective duration of the adherence gain must be long.

The durability problem: how long does brief education last?

This is the most critical empirical gap in the entire case. The value of a single caregiver education session depends entirely on how long its adherence effect persists.

No RCT was identified that tests a single brief (≤1 hour) educational session for NCD medication adherence in a low- or lower-middle-income country with follow-up of 12 months or longer. This gap is itself the most important finding from the literature review. The evidence that exists is fragmentary and mostly pessimistic.

The closest analogues suggest effects are modest and decay within months. A UAE trial (30-minute session, MMAS-8 measure) showed a ~20 percentage-point improvement at 6 months but had no 12-month follow-up. An Indonesian quasi-experiment with brief pharmacist counselling showed a strong immediate effect but the authors themselves described it as effective for only "up to two months." A Brazilian RCT showing strong 6-month results required monthly pharmacist visits — not a single session. A Sri Lankan hospital-based education intervention showed a 22.5 percentage-point adherence improvement at 12 months, but this involved multiple sessions and frequent follow-up.

The literature on multi-session programs suggests that effects sustained at 12 months require at least quarterly booster contact. Single-session effects appear to decay significantly within 3–6 months in the absence of reinforcement. The theoretical case for the caregiver model being more durable than direct patient education — the idea that a family member provides ongoing daily reminders — is coherent but entirely untested at 12-month follow-up in this context.

This decay problem is the most important design question Noora faces. The WhatsApp follow-up service is potentially the solution, but it is optional (families must opt in) and its effect on sustained adherence has not been separately evaluated. If the adherence gain from the hospital session decays to zero within 3 months, and the WhatsApp service reaches only 30% of families, the effective duration of benefit for the average patient is much shorter than the 12-month window typically used in cost-effectiveness models.

For modeling purposes, a conservative assumption of 3-month effective benefit duration, a central assumption of 6 months, and an optimistic assumption of 12 months produces very different cost-effectiveness numbers. At 3 months, even a large adherence effect generates few DALYs per patient. At 12 months, the numbers become more attractive. The truth is unknown.

Secondary prevention: where the real value is

The DALY math looks very different for post-stroke, post-MI, and post-cardiac-surgery patients. This cohort may represent Noora's highest-value opportunity — and it is the cohort that Noora's hospital-based model naturally reaches.

Post-stroke patients in India face a 13% first-year recurrent stroke rate and approximately 28% 3-month mortality, based on national registry data. Post-MI patients face 30-day mortality of 5.6–8.6% (STEMI), 1-year mortality in the 14–18% range. The drug adherence picture is catastrophic: the PURE study documented that in India, only 25% of post-event patients are on antiplatelet therapy, 15% on statins, and 2.5% on three or more recommended secondary-prevention drugs at follow-up. This drug gap is a major driver of India's CVD mortality burden.

Converting the secondary prevention benefit to DALYs produces a dramatically different number. A post-stroke or post-MI patient who achieves full secondary prevention drug adherence has their recurrence risk reduced by approximately 25–35% depending on drug combination. At India's event rates and DALY weights, this translates to approximately 0.27–0.36 DALYs per incremental adherent patient-year — roughly 10–13 times the value of an adherent primary-care hypertension patient-year (0.02 DALYs).

At a $125/DALY threshold, the maximum defensible spend per secondary prevention patient-year is $34–45, compared to $2.50–3.00 for primary hypertension. A $1 caregiver education session easily clears the secondary prevention bar even under pessimistic assumptions about durability. Under central assumptions, secondary prevention is where Noora's cost-effectiveness is genuinely competitive with the best health interventions globally.

Noora already has a cardiac surgery caregiver program. The question is whether the NCD adherence program specifically reaches post-stroke and post-MI patients before discharge from district hospitals — or whether it is primarily capturing newly diagnosed primary-care patients. If the former, the cost-effectiveness profile is strong. If the latter, it is much weaker.

The counterfactual: what government systems actually provide

Noora's marginal value depends on what patients would receive without it. The answer, across all three countries, is stark: for the vast majority of diagnosed NCD patients in government facilities, structured adherence counselling is essentially absent. The realistic counterfactual is "prescription plus brief verbal instruction plus abandonment."

In India, the NP-NCD program operating through Health and Wellness Centres mandates counselling, but implementation is thin. NFHS-5 shows only 11% of hypertensives nationally have controlled BP. The India Hypertension Control Initiative (IHCI) is genuinely better — 44% control among enrolled patients — but covers less than 2% of the hypertensive population. The typical HWC patient gets a BP measurement, a prescription, and 2–3 minutes of verbal instruction. ASHA workers have chronic disease follow-up tasks in their job descriptions but receive no specialized NCD counselling training. Twelve-month treatment retention in the general government system is approximately 50% — meaning half of patients started on antihypertensives have stopped within a year.

Bangladesh presents the weakest government counterfactual of the three countries. The BHCI (Bangladesh Hypertension Control Initiative) is newer and covers a smaller fraction of facilities. Community clinic NCD corners exist but are poorly resourced. Twelve-month retention data from national programs sits around 12% — meaning 88% of patients initiated on treatment have left the system within a year. The counterfactual in Bangladesh is essentially zero meaningful adherence support.

Indonesia is split. Roughly 5% of hypertensive patients are enrolled in Prolanis (Program Pengelolaan Penyakit Kronis), which provides monthly structured visits at Puskesmas with medication review and a group education component. Prolanis patients receive genuinely good adherence support — the program was modelled on European chronic disease management and has documented effectiveness. But the 95% of diagnosed hypertensives not enrolled in Prolanis receive essentially nothing beyond a prescription. The counterfactual for the majority is as weak as Bangladesh. For Prolanis-enrolled patients, a Noora-style add-on would be duplicative.

This counterfactual analysis has a direct implication for country prioritization. Noora's marginal value is highest where the government counterfactual is weakest. That ranking is: Bangladesh first, India second, Indonesia third (weighted average of a strong Prolanis minority and a weak non-Prolanis majority).

Benchmarks: where does a $1 counseling layer sit?

Published cost-effectiveness estimates for NCD interventions in South and Southeast Asia span a wide range, from cost-saving to several thousand dollars per DALY. The most useful comparison points:

  • WHO-CHOICE hypertension treatment (≥25% CVD risk, drug + delivery): approximately Int$47/DALY in Southeast Asia — this is the macro benchmark for treating diagnosed patients
  • IHCI India (70% coverage/adherence scenario): modelled as cost-saving; at 60% coverage, $3.97/DALY — but this includes the drugs, the training, and the protocol infrastructure, not just a counselling add-on
  • Polypill for secondary prevention in India: approximately Int$154/DALY (range 57–289) at public drug pricing
  • COBRA-BPS community health worker follow-up program (lifetime horizon): $252/DALY — this is a strong CHW program with quarterly visits, not a single session
  • SMS reminders for hypertension adherence: $411–4,710/DALY depending on assumptions — digital reminders are structurally more expensive per DALY than human contact because the effect size on clinical outcomes is modest
  • Pharmacist-led adherence counselling: $2,620–4,710/DALY in published LMIC CEAs, primarily because of staff costs

A ~$1/patient caregiver education session that produces a meaningful and durable adherence improvement sits structurally at the low-cost end of this landscape. The modelled estimate for a brief counselling add-on — assuming a 10–16 percentage-point adherence improvement and 6-month effective duration — falls in the $27–336/DALY range. Under optimistic assumptions (12-month duration, larger effect), it competes with the best interventions globally. Under pessimistic assumptions (3-month duration, smaller effect), it is still competitive with SMS programs and pharmacist counselling, though not with direct treatment delivery.

The critical advantage of Noora's model relative to all other adherence interventions on this list is that the marginal cost is near zero once the Master Trainer infrastructure is in place. The $1/patient figure already bakes in that most of the delivery cost is borne by the government. This makes the cost-effectiveness calculation relatively robust to moderate pessimism about effect size — even a small effect at near-zero marginal cost clears most thresholds.

The MARS problem: how credible is Noora's evidence?

Noora reports 70% versus 52% medication adherence for hypertension and 69% versus 55% for diabetes, measured using MARS in one Indian state. This is the primary evidence offered for the NCD program's effectiveness. Several concerns bear on its interpretation.

The study is entirely unpublished as of October 2026. No preprint, conference abstract, IDinsight report, or annual report excerpt containing these specific figures was found after extensive searching. What can be reconstructed from public sources suggests the study was conducted in Punjab (Moga/Ferozpur districts) starting around 2022, using trained enumerators administering the MARS instrument. The comparison group structure — concurrent controlled, historical, or no control — could not be determined from public sources.

The MARS (Medication Adherence Report Scale) was developed in the UK for asthma patients and has never been validated in India or South Asia for hypertension or diabetes populations. A 2026 Delphi study explicitly concluded that no validated adherence tool exists for the Indian context. This is not a minor caveat — instrument validity is foundational to interpreting the results.

The self-report inflation problem is significant. In the global literature, self-reported adherence overstates objectively measured adherence (pill counts, pharmacy refills, MEMS caps) by a median of 17 percentage points. In South Asian clinic populations specifically, social desirability bias is well-documented — patients in Indian government health facilities reliably report higher adherence than they demonstrate on objective measures. Studies comparing MMAS-8 and refill-based adherence in India have found gaps of 25 percentage points or more. If we apply the conservative 17-point inflation adjustment, the "true" adherence levels implied by Noora's MARS results might be approximately 53% (intervention) versus 35% (control) — an 18-point gap that is directionally consistent but measured on a different, lower absolute scale than reported.

The direction of the effect — intervention patients have higher adherence than controls — is probably real. The magnitude is almost certainly overstated. And without knowing the study design, we cannot assess whether selection bias, Hawthorne effects, or regression to the mean explain part of the gap. The result is not credible for funding decisions as currently presented. What would make it credible: a published protocol, objective adherence co-measurement (refill data or pill counts at a subset of sites), and transparent comparison-group baseline data.

The Bangladesh NCD evaluation, which is underway with a more rigorous design, will be the most important piece of evidence Noora produces in the next two years.

The case-finding upside: an untapped opportunity

Noora's caregiver touchpoint creates an opportunity that the current program does not exploit: screening the accompanying family member for undiagnosed NCD.

The case-finding yield would be high. Across India, Bangladesh, and Indonesia, 45–70% of hypertensives are undiagnosed. NCDs cluster within households: a 2025 analysis of 450,520 Indian households from NFHS-5 found that adults living with a hypertensive household member had a 2.49 times higher prevalence of diagnosed hypertension than the general population. Family members accompanying a diagnosed NCD patient to hospital are a selected population with substantially elevated NCD prevalence. A simple BP check and fingerstick glucose test administered to the caregiver during the education session would be expected to detect new hypertension in 25–35% and elevated glucose in 10–15% of screened adults.

A Bangladesh screening study found the cost of community-based opportunistic screening at $1 per person, yielding hypertension detection at $4.93/case — the lowest documented cost per case of any modality studied. At Noora's scale (several million caregiver sessions annually), even modest screening integration could generate 2–3 million new NCD diagnoses per year.

The critical caveat is what happens after detection. Screening without treatment linkage is not impact. The literature on facility-based opportunistic screening shows that 12-month retention of newly detected patients — those who actually get initiated on treatment and remain on it — is typically 20–40%. Building the linkage pathway is harder than the screen itself, and Noora's current program does not include it. Whether this is a gap to fill or a scope limitation to accept depends on Noora's theory of change and government partnership capacity.

Digital nudging as complement, not substitute

A natural question is whether WhatsApp or SMS reminders could extend the durability of brief education effects. The evidence suggests they can — modestly — but with important limits.

The strongest LMIC trial of automated SMS for hypertension is StAR (South Africa, N=1,372, 12 months), which found that weekly automated messages reduced SBP by 2.2 mmHg versus usual care. This is clinically meaningful but modest — roughly equivalent to one-fifth of a standard antihypertensive. For HbA1c (diabetes), the evidence across multiple trials is consistently null. SMS reminders do not improve glycaemic control, probably because diabetes management requires complex multi-behaviour change that simple cues cannot drive.

The engagement decay problem is significant. Open rates and response rates in digital health programs typically decline by 40–60% within 6 months. Most programs that show strong early effects show attenuated effects by 12 months. What happens when messages stop is even clearer: effects largely disappear. Digital reminders are a temporally bounded intervention — they produce adherence gains only during active delivery.

Indonesia's WhatsApp penetration (~65% of the population, 180M users) makes it a particularly promising context for digital extension. The MaRiTensi trial from Indonesia showed a WhatsApp-based app improved BP monitoring adherence over 12 weeks. No trial from Indonesia with 12-month clinical outcomes currently exists.

The theoretical framing that best fits the evidence: human education builds knowledge and motivation — slow to install but potentially durable because it changes how a person understands their condition. Digital reminders provide real-time cues — fast to install but requiring ongoing delivery. They are complements, not substitutes. A Noora session followed by an opt-in WhatsApp booster service is theoretically stronger than either alone. The relative contribution of each has not been experimentally isolated.

The Indonesia steelman

Of the three countries where Noora operates or is considering expansion, Indonesia presents the most structurally compelling case for an adherence-focused NCD program — despite the fact that Noora does not yet have an NCD program there.

The cascade argument is first. Indonesia's hypertension cascade breaks specifically at the control stage rather than at awareness or treatment initiation. Among diagnosed and treated Indonesians, 83% are on treatment — but only 19% achieve controlled BP. This pattern implies that the constraint on health outcomes is not diagnosis or drug access (Jaminan Kesehatan Nasional covers nearly all antihypertensives for the 95% of Indonesians with JKN coverage) but something in the treatment experience that prevents achievement of control. Medication adherence and medication intensification are the primary candidates.

JKN coverage also purifies the adherence problem in a way that does not hold in India or Bangladesh. In India, cost is a meaningful contributor to non-adherence — patients skip doses when out of pocket spending is high. In Indonesia, drugs are free for JKN enrollees. The residual non-adherence is therefore more purely behavioral and knowledge-based — exactly what caregiver education addresses.

Indonesian hospital culture provides a structural advantage for Noora's model. The concept of keluarga penunggu — an accompanying family member who stays with the patient during hospitalization — is institutionalized in Indonesian public hospitals in a way that is even more embedded than in Indian tertiary facilities. Family members are expected to be present; hospitals have designated spaces for them. The friction of getting family to participate in a caregiver education session is lower than anywhere else in the region.

Finally, BPJS Kesehatan (the national health insurer) maintains administrative claims data covering 95% of the population, including every drug dispensed, every outpatient visit, and every hospitalization. An evaluation of Noora's program in Indonesia could use refill claims as an objective adherence proxy — eliminating the MARS reliability problem entirely. This makes Indonesia not just a compelling program market but the most credible evaluation market in Noora's potential portfolio.

Verdict

Bottom line

Noora Health's NCD Care Companion Program is a cheap, scalable intervention addressing a genuine adherence gap in markets with weak government counterfactuals. The cost-effectiveness case is defensible under central assumptions but rests on three empirical uncertainties that are not yet resolved: how long the adherence gain from a single session persists, how much of Noora's MARS-measured effect survives adjustment to objective adherence, and whether the program is primarily reaching secondary prevention patients (where the DALY value is very high) or primary-care NCD patients (where it is much lower).

Funding is warranted at current scale as a program investment, conditional on: (1) the Bangladesh NCD evaluation proceeding with objective adherence co-measurement alongside MARS; (2) Noora reporting explicitly on the share of NCD patients who are secondary prevention (post-stroke, post-MI, post-surgery) versus primary-care; (3) a plan to test whether the WhatsApp follow-up service materially extends adherence duration.

Indonesia is the strongest expansion case: favorable cascade shape, JKN removes cost barrier, institutional family presence in hospitals, and BPJS data enables rigorous evaluation. A pilot in 10–15 district hospitals in one province, designed as a cluster RCT using BPJS refill data as the primary outcome, would produce the highest-quality evidence in Noora's portfolio and could decisively resolve the durability question within 24 months.

The ceiling on cost-effectiveness is determined by program targeting. If even 20–30% of Noora's NCD patients are secondary prevention cases, the portfolio average DALY value rises substantially and the intervention becomes competitive with GiveDirectly at the $50–125/DALY range. If the program is almost entirely primary hypertension/diabetes in newly diagnosed patients with a 3-month benefit horizon, it likely sits in the $300–800/DALY range — still fundable, but not a top-tier global health priority.

Key open questions in order of importance

  • Durability: What is the adherence trajectory at 6 and 12 months post-session, with and without WhatsApp follow-up? This is the single highest-value empirical question.
  • Patient mix: What fraction of NCD program patients are secondary prevention versus primary NCD? A simple tagging exercise on existing records could answer this quickly.
  • MARS validation: Publish the Punjab study protocol and data. Commission a concurrent objective adherence sub-study (even 200 patients with refill tracking) to calibrate the MARS-to-objective gap in this specific population.
  • Case-finding: Pilot a BP check for accompanying family members at a subset of sites. Estimate yield and linkage rate before committing to integration.
  • Indonesia: Explore MoU with one provincial health system for a pilot evaluation using BPJS data.
