One Acre Fund — Agroforestry

From one tree to a DALY

A bottom-up account of OAF's agroforestry cost-effectiveness, the total addressable market at <$150/DALY, and the case for a $25–30M/yr commitment.

Bottom line

OAF's Kenya and Burundi tree programs deliver $73–$93/DALY (income + corrected carbon), making them among the most cost-effective livelihood interventions with RCT validation. Current FP recommendation is $12.6M/yr. The near-term defensible ceiling is ~$25–30M/yr — roughly double — once Malawi is evaluated and Kenya expanded. Nigeria is the long-run option bet but not bankable before 2029–2030.

The BOTEC contains one material error that FP's published model does not correct: using 0.00957 DALY/tCO2 (includes a 31% air-pollution co-benefit inapplicable to tree planting). Corrected to 0.00658, Kenya is $93 not $85, and Ethiopia is $126 not $113 — still below $150, but the margin on Ethiopia is thinner than claimed.

The mechanism

OAF distributes tree seedlings to smallholder farmers alongside its core input package (seeds, fertilizer, credit). Farmers plant trees on farm boundaries and intercrop rows. Over 15 years, trees provide: timber and poles (primary income), firewood, soil nitrogen fixation (for companion species like Calliandra), and carbon sequestration.

The primary species is Grevillea robusta (~55% of Kenya trees), a fast-growing hardwood native to Australia. Secondary species include Eucalyptus (~25%), Markhamia lutea, and Casuarina. In Malawi and Nigeria, the mix shifts toward fruit trees (mango, citrus, cashew) with higher NPV but slower income realization.

The causal chain from intervention to DALY has six links, each with its own empirical evidence and uncertainty:

BOTEC chain: one tree → one DALY

1
Tree distributed. OAF distributes seedlings through ~4,000 entrepreneur-run nurseries. Farmers receive a kit of seedlings + agronomic advice bundled with the main input loan. Distribution cost is ~$0.20–0.35/tree including nursery production, logistics, and field staff.

2
Tree survives. Survival rate: Kenya 61% · Rwanda 59% · Burundi 54% · Malawi ~30% · Ethiopia ~70%. Kenya's 61% is validated by the Laterite 2020 RCT (+11 grevillea trees per treatment farmer at 2-year follow-up). Malawi's low rate — drought, termites, narrow 5-species portfolio — is the program's biggest weakness.

Counterfactual adjustment. Not all surviving trees are additional. BOTEC applies a counterfactual fraction: some farmers would have planted trees anyway. OAF's RCT design estimates the counterfactual through control group behavior (3.4% crossover in Kenya RCT — very low). For simplicity the BOTEC treats all RCT-measured trees as additional.

Carbon co-benefit

Carbon sequestration adds ~20% to OAF's cost-effectiveness (not the 28% claimed by the BOTEC). The correction matters but does not change the bottom-line recommendation.

Material error in published BOTEC
The BOTEC uses 0.00957 DALY/tCO2 for the carbon conversion. This figure includes a 31.2% component for avoided air pollution from fossil fuel displacement — which does not apply to tree planting. Trees sequester atmospheric CO2; they do not displace any combustion. The correct figure is 0.00658 DALY/tCO2 (GHG-only health damage pathway). This reduces the carbon improvement from ~28% to ~20%.

Permanence (28%)

OAF trees are harvested, not left standing. End use: ~40% firewood (combusted immediately, zero permanence), ~30% poles (10–15yr half-life), ~15% timber, ~15% left standing. Under tonne-year accounting (credit storage proportional to 100yr benchmark), the BOTEC's 28% permanence reconstructs accurately. Under IPCC HWP endpoint accounting, ~41% of carbon persists at 15yr — actually higher than the BOTEC claims. The tonne-year method is conservative on this dimension.

What drives the variance?

The dominant uncertainty is not the carbon science — it is the philosophical choice of how to value a tonne of CO2 in health terms. DALY/tCO2 conversion alone swings Kenya's combined $/DALY by $53 (from $116 to $63). The actual carbon parameters — gross sequestration and permanence — each swing it by $36–41. This means carbon due diligence should focus on the DALY/tCO2 assumption, not on measuring more trees.

The carbon co-benefit is a meaningful bonus (~20% improvement) but cannot by itself bring OAF into GiveWell territory. Adding carbon does not change OAF's tier.

Why Kenya's CE deteriorated 70% in one year

In October 2025, FP estimated Kenya at 10× cash ($0.27/surviving tree). By September 2026, that had fallen to 7× cash ($0.46/tree) — a 70% cost increase driven by two factors: (1) a large climate philanthropist pulled out in 2026, switching to pure carbon removal, which caused the program to contract and spread fixed costs over fewer trees; (2) OAF rebudgeted ~35% more operational costs to the tree program unit. The program quality did not change. The donor pullout directly caused the CE deterioration.

This also implies that marginal cost of additional trees is much lower than average cost. Fixed costs (tech/IT, finance, HR, offices) are ~$3–4M/yr and don't scale with tree volume. Variable costs (production, distribution, seasonal staff) are ~$2M/yr. A new funder effectively buys down the average by growing the denominator.

Malawi has the worst survival in OAF's portfolio (4.7 vs Kenya's 18.3) but may already be ~$90–120/DALY due to its sheer scale efficiency: 560,000 farmers means delivery cost per farmer is low, partially compensating for poor trees-per-adopter. The CE reconciliation: even 4.7 trees × $6.66 NPV × 0.36 validity + carbon / ~$1.50/adopter cost ≈ $90–120/DALY.

Why survival is terrible: (1) unimodal rainfall — seedlings planted late face 5–6 dry months before establishment; (2) termites (farmers' #1 cited cause); (3) narrow 5-species portfolio; (4) seedling quality issues. OAF targets 65% survival by 2030 from ~30% now.

If survival improves to 8 trees/adopter (still below Kenya), Malawi would be the BEST country in the portfolio at ~$74/DALY. The species improvement pathway is clear: Gliricidia thrives under drought; mango and citrus trials underway; macadamia has strong export demand.

Nigeria is where the optimistic TAM scenario lives or dies. OAF distributes cashew, mango, citrus, oil palm, Moringa in Niger, Kwara, and Nasarawa states, targeting 4M farmers by 2030. Cashew NPV in Guinea Savanna could be $50–200/tree (major export crop) vs grevillea's $6.66 — potentially transformative for CE.

But: zero published survival data, zero independent CE evaluation, 2-year-old program at scale, single rainy season with harsh Harmattan dry period. If cashew and mango survive at 40%+, Nigeria unlocks 1–4M farmers at ~$150/DALY and $20–80M/yr RFF. If not: zero.

The $12.6M FP recommendation is a genuine lower bound. A defensible near-term commitment is ~$25–30M/yr: the current $12.6M plus ~$5–10M for Malawi evaluation and scale-up plus ~$5–10M for Kenya expansion to 1.5M farmers plus ~$3–5M for Ethiopia evidence (if politically stable). Against the Scenario B ceiling of $28–42M/yr, the practical 2027 ask is about half of total potential.

Absorptive capacity

OAF pre-commits program scale ~1 year in advance (seedling growing lead time). Historical growth is 20–25%/yr. From the current $12.6M recommended base, organic absorption would grow to ~$20M by 2028 and ~$31M by 2030. An accelerated commitment of $25–30M/yr from 2027 would require OAF to expand its nursery network and staff ~18 months early — feasible but requires forward commitment now.

OAF's organizational ceiling for the tree program is ~$60–80M/yr total spend. The Scenario B ceiling of $28–42M/yr is well within that range. Scenario C ($51–127M) would require a step-change in organizational capacity, unlikely before 2031+.

What to fund and when

Immediate (2027)

  1. Kenya + Burundi at $12.6M/yr — the no-brainer. RCT-validated, clear threshold, absorptive capacity confirmed.
  2. Malawi evaluation (~$0.5–1M) — highest value-of-information spend in the entire OAF portfolio. Confirms whether the CE estimate is real and whether the survival improvement pathway is credible.
  3. Kenya expansion to 1.5M farmers (+$5–10M/yr) — riding down the fixed cost curve; CE improves as fixed costs spread over more trees.

Near-term (2028–2029)

  1. Malawi scale-up ($5–10M/yr) if Malawi evaluation is positive — single largest expansion opportunity.
  2. Ethiopia evidence ($3M/yr) — commission NPV validation study and post-conflict operational assessment. Do not scale before results.
  3. Burundi to 500k farmers (+$2–4M/yr).

Medium-term (2030+)

  1. Nigeria — if 3-year survival data shows cashew/mango viability, Nigeria becomes the biggest prize. Don't commit large dollars until outcome data exists.
  2. Ethiopia at scale (if political stability and NPV both confirmed) — +$3–5M/yr.
  3. Tanzania only if fruit tree diversification succeeds at pilot scale.

What NOT to fund

  • Uganda — wrong model, wrong economics
  • Zambia at scale — Faidherbia too slow, won't clear $150
  • Nigeria at scale before survival data (3+ years out)
  • Beyond-OAF organizations (TFTF, Vi Agroforestry, FMNR) without independent CE evaluation matching FP standard
  • OAF's core crop program — different CE profile, different evaluation

Risks and evidence gaps

The single most important unknown

Malawi survival trajectory. If OAF achieves its 65% survival target by 2030 (from ~30% now), Malawi goes from the worst to the best country in the portfolio. If survival stays stuck, Malawi never enters the TAM. One study, $0.5–1M, would answer this with enough certainty to commit or pass on a $5–10M/yr line.
