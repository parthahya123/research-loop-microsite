NeoTest: A $60M Bet on Neonatal Sepsis Diagnostics

A proposed $60M advance market commitment would incentivize development of a rapid blood test for newborn sepsis. We spent 62 research iterations — plus a formal back-and-forth with the lead modeller at CGD — stress-testing whether it's worth it. Here are our biggest unresolved concerns.

Jargon guide: DALY = one year of healthy life lost to death or disability. $/DALY = cost per healthy year saved; GiveWell's bar for "exceptional" is ~$50–100. AMC = Advance Market Commitment. SNCU = Special Newborn Care Unit. PSBI = Possible Serious Bacterial Infection. Se/Sp = sensitivity/specificity.

The problem

Every year, roughly 3 million newborns in low- and middle-income countries show signs of serious bacterial infection — fever, fast breathing, feeding problems, limpness. Sepsis kills around 680,000 of them. It is one of the leading causes of neonatal death globally, and disproportionately concentrated in South Asia and sub-Saharan Africa.

The core clinical difficulty is that most of these babies look similar whether they are actually septic or just transiently unwell. The gold standard diagnostic — a blood culture that identifies bacteria — takes 48–72 hours to return a result, requires lab infrastructure that most LMIC hospitals don't have, and misses roughly 45% of true cases (bacteria don't always grow). So doctors face a daily choice with bad options on both sides: treat everyone who looks sick (over-treatment, antibiotic side effects, resistance), or wait for more information (under-treatment, preventable deaths).

In practice, most LMIC hospitals choose a third path: treat based on WHO "danger signs" — a clinical checklist that is deliberately sensitive but not specific. Among neonates who meet danger-sign criteria, roughly 40–80% actually have sepsis (the rest are sick for other reasons or will recover without antibiotics). Empiric antibiotic rates at Indian specialist newborn units run 70–80% of all admissions. The result: a majority of babies admitted to these units receive antibiotics they may not need, while a subset who genuinely need them may not get them fast enough.

This is the problem a rapid diagnostic test would solve — at the bedside, in 15 minutes, before any treatment decision is made.

The proposed solution

NeoTest is a lateral-flow immunoassay — structurally similar to a COVID rapid antigen test, but calibrated for newborn sepsis biomarkers. A clinician takes a small blood sample (finger-prick or heel-prick), mixes it with a reagent, and reads the result on a strip in 10–20 minutes. No lab equipment. No cold chain. Cost at scale: ~$3–7 per test.

The test would work in two clinical modes:

  • Initiation-gate (most valuable): At facilities where doctors don't automatically treat every sick neonate — district hospitals, health centres, private clinics — a positive test triggers antibiotic treatment. Without the test, ~40–60% of these babies would go untreated. The test catches the ones who genuinely need treatment. This is where most of the lives-saved math comes from.
  • De-escalation (also valuable): At specialist newborn units (SNCUs) where empiric antibiotics are the default, a negative test result at 48–72 hours gives clinicians confidence to stop treatment. This shortens unnecessary antibiotic courses, reduces hospital-acquired infections, and is the dominant mode at India's highest-volume facilities.

No qualifying test currently exists. NeoSep-SAA (a consortium including FIND and SD Biosensor) is the most advanced candidate; a pivotal trial in Uganda is underway. WHO has published a target product profile: at minimum 90% sensitivity and 70% specificity on neonatal capillary blood. Whether the existing candidates will hit that threshold on the blood sample type used in real deployments is the primary technical uncertainty.

If a qualifying test is developed and reaches patients, the downstream treatment is antibiotics — which already exist and are cheap. NeoTest is not a new treatment; it is a routing tool that gets the right treatment to the right babies faster, and stops the wrong treatment in babies who don't need it.

The investment mechanism

No company has invested seriously in developing an affordable neonatal sepsis rapid test for LMIC markets. The reason is straightforward: the customers who most need it can't pay much for it. Developing a novel diagnostic costs $50–150M in R&D; selling it at $3–5/test to underfunded LMIC health systems doesn't cover that investment. This is a classic market failure — a product that would save many lives sits undeveloped because the economics don't work without external intervention.

The proposed solution is an Advance Market Commitment (AMC): a philanthropic funder pre-commits to subsidise the purchase of qualifying tests once they exist. Specifically:

  • $20M milestone prize: Paid to the first company that achieves WHO prequalification and meets the TPP — enough to recoup late-stage development costs.
  • $40M in per-test subsidies: Paid out as tests are sold to LMIC health systems — $7/test for the first tranche, stepping down to $5 and $3 as volume grows. This bridges the gap between what manufacturers need (~$7–10/test) and what LMIC health systems can pay (~$1–3/test).
  • Total commitment: $60M — but only disbursed if a qualifying test is developed and bought. If no test qualifies, the funder pays $0.

The AMC model has precedent: the Gavi pneumococcal AMC (2009, $1.5B) successfully accelerated PCV vaccine access to low-income countries by roughly a decade. The structural analogy is sound. The key question is whether the NeoTest AMC is designed well enough to work — and our analysis finds six specific design flaws that need fixing before any commitment.

Important nuance for the CE math: Because the AMC only pays out on success, the cost-per-DALY calculation is conditional on a qualifying test being developed. You're not paying ~$200–400/DALY on a bet that might not pay off — you're paying ~$200–400/DALY in the world where the bet worked. The probability of success affects whether you should commit, not the $/DALY figure itself.

Akhil leads the CGD team that built the original model. He provided a formal written rebuttal to our analysis and a second round of follow-up. We've been genuinely updating each other — and after running three dedicated fact-check research loops on his specific claims, we accept two of his corrections.

The 0.1665 DALYs/test figure is already the marginal effect of the test alone. Applying ×2.5 was double-discounting. Retracted.

The model's 30% is total TAM penetration across all settings — facility-based. Community-presenting babies go to PHCs/CHCs/district hospitals for testing; CHWs don't administer the test. Our framing was wrong. Retracted.
  • How the test works in hospitals. We initially worried about harm from withholding antibiotics. Akhil showed the model targets the ~40–50% of hospital newborns in the clinical grey zone — positive test initiates treatment, doesn't replace it. Withdrawn.
  • The test can reach more than just specialist newborn wards. District hospitals, private newborn units, and PHCs add ~2.65× deployable volume. Incorporated.

The one remaining genuine dispute: adoption speed

After retracting corrections 1 and 4, the adoption correction (correction 3) is now the dominant driver of the gap between CGD's $48 and our ~$169. The question is not whether the model correctly defines its parameters — it does — but whether the S-curve speed is calibrated correctly.

CGD calibrates the adoption curve to malaria RDTs (~48% of febrile children in Africa by year 8) and GeneXpert (which Akhil reads as ~47% of TB diagnoses globally). We agree these are the right analogues. The dispute is whether NeoTest's diffusion speed will match them.

Why we maintain a ×2.0 speed correction. GeneXpert's global figure (~47%) masks India-specific performance: India reached ~38% of notified TB patients by 2024 — after 14 years post-WHO-recommendation, with Global Fund subsidized procurement, a dedicated national vertical program (NTEP, est. 1962), and explicit political commitment (PM-TB Mukt Bharat). NeoTest enters without any of these: no WHO recommendation, no procurement channel, no national vertical program, no existing diagnostic pathway for neonatal sepsis.

Malaria RDTs are an even stronger diffusion environment: they address the single most common cause of childhood fever in endemic Africa, are distributed free through national programs, and had a 2010 WHO universal testing mandate. NeoTest addresses a diagnosis that clinicians do not currently test for and that has no mandate, no program budget, and no established pathway.

CGD's S-curve reaches ~30% of PSBI in 5–8 years. The best-resourced comparable LMIC diagnostic — GeneXpert in India — took 14 years with every advantage to reach ~38%. A ×2.0 correction on cumulative volume within the model window is the honest central; ×1.5 is optimistic but possible if the AMC creates a genuine pull.

Structural prerequisites analysis: India has achieved >15% POCT adoption exactly three times (malaria RDTs ~90%, HIV rapid tests ~77%, GeneXpert ~30–35%). Every case required all five prerequisites simultaneously: (1) vertical disease program, (2) central government mandate, (3) dedicated NHM budget line, (4) free supply via centralized procurement, (5) pre-existing clinical workflow. NeoTest has 0 of 5. SNCU-only deployment saturates at ~37% of deployable TAM — getting to 20% total requires sub-SNCU deployment, which faces severe infrastructure gaps (50% regular electricity, 86.6% nurse deficit vs norms). 20–25% is achievable but requires ~$10–15M in deliberate adoption support above the AMC, with the first procurement contracts not appearing until ~2034–35 at earliest.

The live dispute

Adoption speed — accounts for ~2× of the remaining gap, genuinely uncertain

Akhil calibrates the S-curve to malaria RDTs (48% of febrile children in Africa by year 8) and GeneXpert globally (~47% of TB diagnoses). We agree these are right analogues. The dispute: CGD assumes NeoTest will diffuse at comparable speed, but GeneXpert's India-specific performance was ~38% after 14 years — with every structural advantage NeoTest lacks. We apply a ×2.0 correction on S-curve speed (range ×1.5–2.5). Akhil disputes any correction.

What resolves this: first Kenya/Uganda deployment data (expected 2026–27). If adoption tracks >15% of PSBI infants within 3 years, Akhil's calibration holds and CE improves to ~$55–80/DALY. If it tracks slower (<10% after 3 years), our correction is right. This is the single most important empirical question for NeoTest's CE.

Timeline — partially settled; Akhil accepts 2yr slip

Akhil now accepts ~2 years of slippage from his 2031 base case. Three independent research runs converge on ~2037 India public-sector uptake. Residual dispute: whether Akhil's ICMR working group participation creates any binding commitment — it doesn't.

Unresolved concern: DALYs/test — are the 0.1665 DALYs/test credible in practice?

This is the concern that was lurking behind our retracted leverage correction and is more important than adoption speed. The CGD HTA's 0.1665 DALYs/test is incremental relative to a specific clinical counterfactual: that clinicians in the SOC arm miss ~44% of true sepsis cases and send them home untreated. Eight dedicated research runs interrogated whether this counterfactual is correct.

What the evidence shows: The answer depends entirely on which patients the test is deployed in.

  • ICMR Tier 1 (danger-sign presenting neonates): f_cf ≈ 0.87–0.95 — essentially all receive empiric antibiotics by FBNC/ICMR protocol regardless of diagnostic test. The test's Mode A (initiation-gate) value here is near zero to net-harmful. Mode B (de-escalation) gives ~0.003 DALYs/test. Indian SNCUs do NOT use the H/M/L risk stratification the HTA assumes — they treat empirically on danger signs.
  • ICMR Tier 2 / grey zone (~40–50% of SNCU admissions): f_cf ≈ 0.30–0.60 (central ~0.45). The ICMR guideline says to observe without treating; in practice ~half get antibiotics. This is the primary deployment target and where the test has genuine initiation value. The CGD's f_cf = 0.56 is approximately right for this population.

But two concerns survive even at grey-zone f_cf: (1) Will clinicians in practice correctly target grey-zone cases rather than danger-sign cases? If the test bleeds into Tier 1 use, the initiation math flips negative. (2) NNF Recommendation 9 explicitly discourages stopping antibiotics based on negative biomarker results for culture-negative probable sepsis — this limits the de-escalation channel that the HTA credits. Maheshwara NICU showed both channels can work in practice, but it is one tertiary centre.

Bottom line: The test has genuine but modest value in the grey zone. Honest DALYs/test for grey-zone-targeted deployment: ~0.07–0.12 (vs HTA's 0.1665) — a ~1.5–2× haircut. This is a parameter adjustment, not a sign flip, IF the test is correctly targeted. Add this to the adoption correction (×2.0) and timeline correction (×1.15), and the revised range including the DALYs/test concern is ~$200–400/DALY (from the current ~$110–170 that excluded this concern). Still within WHO India threshold (~$1,400) but not GiveWell-competitive.

What we now know — and what remains open

Since publishing the two-crux framing above, three dedicated research runs have substantially resolved the regulatory timeline crux. The adoption rate crux remains genuinely open.

Regulatory timeline crux — now largely resolved against Akhil's 2031 claim

Uganda trial deep dive: There is no registered, funded, or actively-enrolling pivotal trial for NeoSep-SAA as of September 2026. The only published data is a single PLOS ONE study (n=714) using a clinical/syndromic reference standard, not the culture-confirmed standard WHO-PQ requires. P(WHO-PQ approval ≤2030) ≈ 0.000. P(≤2035) ≈ 0.47.

India procurement case studies: Empirical review of 8 diagnostics India has deployed shows the base case for NeoTest public-sector uptake (>5% of SNCUs) is ~2037–38. Fastest plausible ~2035–36. Akhil's 2031 requires emergency/pandemic-like pace for which no non-COVID precedent exists. NeoTest lacks the three accelerators that drove fast adoption in other programs (dedicated vertical program, acutely-recognized care gap, established diagnostic category).

NEDL pre-listing mechanism: NEDL pre-listing is technically feasible (NEDL already lists diagnostic categories, not brands) but operationally near-empty. Saves ~0.5–1.5 years at most — not the 2–4 years Akhil claims. What Akhil has verified: an ICMR senior scientist (Kamini Walia) participates in the CGD working group. What is not verified: any MoHFW commitment letter, any budget-backed procurement pledge, or any timeline guarantee. Working group participation ≠ institutional commitment.

Bottom line: The regulatory timeline correction (2031 → 2037) is now supported by three independent lines of evidence. It is no longer a "disputed" crux — it is a settled correction. Akhil's optimistic case on timeline requires conditions that do not currently exist.

Running the full model with both cruxes resolved in Akhil's favor (2031 India launch, 20–25% adoption) and all settled corrections applied: $299/DALY at STEEL DALYs/test (0.042); $1,255/DALY at conservative DALYs/test (0.010). No revision to the stated range is needed, but the honest framing is: $400–600 requires believing both cruxes resolve in Akhil's favor AND that diagnosis changes treatment outcomes at 2–3× our conservative estimate.

Even in the optimistic scenario, NeoTest is 3–12× worse than GiveWell alternatives (AMF ITNs ~$78/DALY, SMC ~$70–110, KMC ~$40–80). The optimistic case makes it less bad as a DALY buy, not good. The single most important parameter is DALYs/test — not launch timing, not adoption rate. A credible demonstration that faster diagnosis changes treatment decisions in LMIC neonatal settings (i.e., DALYs/test > 0.03) would change the verdict. The dominant residual risk in the optimistic scenario is deployment drift into Mode A (initiation-gating) at SNCUs — where the initiation math flips negative at high f_cf.

The highest-leverage near-term decision point: fund a $1–3M catalytic pivotal trial slice for NeoSep-SAA now (no registered trial exists; without funding it starts in 2028–2029 at best), gate the $10M milestone prize on clean pivotal data with a game-proof 7-element specification (tiered composite reference standard + IMAC + binding LMIC price commitment), and make any further disbursement conditional on India adoption data from year 1–3 deployment. The full $60M commitment is premature.

The five structural differences

1 — Discount rate: 2% vs 9% Works in both directions
Akhil

2% social rate in base model (Summary tab; PV costs: $52.6M). Georgia's conservative run (the $119 CE) uses 4%. Both contrast with our 9% opportunity-cost rate.

Ours

9% cost discount (funder opportunity cost), 4% DALY discount. "What else could this $60M earn?" PV costs: $21.2M.

The discount rate is not a technical parameter — it is a philosophical one. It answers the question: how much less do you value a dollar of impact that arrives in 2040 versus 2030?

A social planner — someone optimizing for collective welfare across time — uses a low rate (2–3%) because future people's lives matter nearly as much as present ones. A funder with an investment portfolio uses a higher rate (8–10%) because the alternative use of that money is a portfolio earning real returns.

The counterintuitive effect: our higher cost-discount rate actually makes our PV costs lower ($21.2M vs Akhil's $52.6M at 2%). Money disbursed as AMC payments in 2035–2045 is worth much less today at 9% than at 2%. But this doesn't improve our CE much, because we also model fewer DALYs (see differences 3 and 4). The cost-discount effect is more than offset by the adoption and DALYs differences.

Who is right? For comparing to GiveWell benchmarks — which use a similar opportunity-cost framing — our 9% is the right rate. For a policy audience arguing for government funding, Akhil's 2% is appropriate. Both can be correct for their intended audience.

2 — What population does the test reach? (TAM definition) Closer than it looks
Akhil

PSBI presentations: all neonates meeting WHO danger-sign criteria, both facility-born and community-presenting. India: ~1.9M/yr. Denominator includes community cases that haven't yet reached a testing facility.

Ours

SNCU (Special Newborn Care Unit — India's specialist neonatal wards) admissions: neonates already in specialist wards. India: 1.9M headline, 265–500k functional (workup-relevant). The TAM is already inside the facility.

At first glance these look like very different populations. In practice, the headline TAM figures are almost identical — both are roughly 1.9M for India. That is not a coincidence: SNCU admissions and PSBI presentations in India are closely related, because ~89% of births are institutional (NFHS-5), and most PSBI cases present to or through the same facilities.

The conceptual difference matters more than the numerical one. Akhil's PSBI frame asks "how many neonates could benefit if this test were widely deployed?" Ours asks "how many are already in a facility where the test could be run today?" The PSBI frame is broader and more appropriate for long-run market sizing; the SNCU frame is more conservative and more appropriate for near-term CE estimation.

Where the two definitions diverge materially: Akhil includes community-presenting neonates who are not in SNCUs. These require both a community-level test deployment AND a functional referral pathway to get treated. Our model captures this separately through the c2 community-adherence parameter — which Akhil's model does not apply (see Difference 4).

Our model's blended 5.5–15% across clusters generates roughly 130,000 India tests/year at peak; Akhil's original (PHC-inclusive TAM) generates up to ~570,000. Sep 2026 call clarification: Akhil uses a dual analog, not just malaria RDT. GeneXpert calibrates the absolute penetration level (more conservative — instrument-based, molecular, requires training and cold chain). Malaria RDT calibrates the slope steepness (rate of ramp-up). The SNCU procurement history is retained as a lower-bound triangulation. This is a more defensible methodology than single-analog — GeneXpert is the right reference for a specialist clinical deployment context.

Even so, NeoTest is simpler than GeneXpert (lateral-flow vs molecular, ambient vs cold-chain storage), which argues for faster adoption curves than GeneXpert achieved. A research loop on the GeneXpert penetration curve — time to 50% peak in India, Nigeria, Ethiopia — is running to validate whether Akhil's penetration parameters are calibrated correctly.

Our calibration to TNMSC and Karnataka tender histories is more specific but less data-rich. The honest range is wide. Akhil's 15% (Georgia conservative) is not implausible over 15 years; our 5.5% for the HB cluster is conservative but appropriate as the starting point for a novel diagnostic without prior SNCU procurement precedent.

Why 5.5% rather than 15%? The India SNCU procurement reality

India's 1.9M SNCU admissions headline includes a long tail of non-functional units — facilities that technically have an SNCU designation but run without adequate staffing, power, or supplies. The workup-relevant denominator (where a clinician would actually use a rapid test result) is closer to 265–500k.

5.5% of the headline 1.9M is 105k tests — which is 21–40% of the corrected functional TAM. In that framing, 5.5% is not conservative; it is ambitious relative to current Indian public-sector CRP testing rates, which run below 10% even in well-equipped SNCUs.

The Leader cluster (AIIMS, JIPMER, KEM) runs at 15% adoption in our model — closer to Akhil's assumption — because these institutions already have functioning microbiological workup protocols and are the most likely early adopters. The difference is that we do not assume Leader-tier adoption generalises rapidly to the HB cluster of district hospitals.

4 — Does community deployment actually work? (DALYs per test) Akhil more optimistic → his CE better
Akhil

0.1665 DALYs/test, taken directly from the HTA preprint. The HTA models a 50/50 inborn/community split with its own internal referral and adherence rates baked in.

Ours

~0.132 DALYs/test after applying c2 = 0.38 community adherence haircut. WHO PSBI programme field data calibrates the referral-adherence central at 0.38, not the 0.50 the HTA implies.

The HTA's community DALY estimate rests on a specific assumption about how often a positive test result in a community setting actually leads to treatment. The HTA uses a referral rate parameter (Ref_M = 30%, Ref_H = 60%) that implies an effective community adherence of roughly 0.45–0.50.

When we calibrate this parameter to WHO PSBI programme field data, the empirical central is 0.38 — about 24% below the HTA's implied 0.50. Since the community arm carries roughly half of modelled DALYs, the effect on total DALYs/test is about 12%: from 0.1665 (Akhil, no c2 adjustment) to approximately 0.132 in our model after applying c2 = 0.38. This is a 21% reduction relative to Akhil's 0.1665.

Akhil's HTA is the only published quantitative framework for this technology — it's the natural starting point. Our c2 haircut is a judgment that real-world community adherence in LMIC settings runs lower than the HTA's parameters assume. The answer is empirical. Field data from the first real deployment will settle it.

What c2 actually means — and why it matters so much

c2 is the community adherence parameter: the fraction of test-positive infants in community settings who actually receive the treatment the test result recommends. It captures everything between "the test gives a positive result" and "the baby gets antibiotics": does the health worker tell the family? Does the family believe it? Can they get to a facility? Does the facility have stock?

In the 50/50 inborn/community model, c2 only applies to the community half. The inborn (SNCU) half has structural adherence near 1.0 — the clinician is already there, the baby is already admitted, the antibiotics are already available.

Moving c2 from 0.50 (HTA implied) to 0.38 (WHO PSBI empirical central) reduces the community mortality stream by about 24%, cutting total DALYs/test from 0.1665 to approximately 0.132 — a 21% reduction. This is the largest single driver of our higher CE versus Akhil's.

After the care-seeking chain analysis we conducted, we confirmed that c2 (clinician/family adherence to test result) is now the binding constraint — not L1/L2 (care-seeking and reaching a facility), which the denominator correction showed was a wrong-denominator artifact. The question is not whether PSBI babies reach a testing facility; in India, 89% of births are institutional. The question is whether a positive result at 48–72 hours changes the treatment decision. Field evidence from Indian NICUs is promising but limited to a few sites.

Two years seems small. But under a pure-acceleration model — where the marginal DALYs are only those that happen earlier because of the AMC — a 2-year shift in launch date moves the starting point of the accumulation window and has a compounding effect through discounting.

Akhil's 2030 regulatory approval requires NeoSep-SAA to complete its culture-confirmed pivotal study, achieve WHO prequalification, and clear CDSCO registration in about four years from now. The regulatory pathway analysis we commissioned found that across 47 novel diagnostics, the median from WHO PQ (prequalification) to national LMIC (Low- and Middle-Income Country) scale is ten years, and the fastest feasible path (embedding Indian SNCU sites in the global pivotal, compressing the NEDL nomination) produces a 2031 NEDL (National Essential Diagnostics List — India's official formulary that triggers government procurement) listing — not a 2030 regulatory clearance.

Sep 2026 call — Akhil's three counterpoints: (1) A $60M facility meaningfully accelerates timelines by improving commercial return visibility, compressing firm R&D cycles. (2) Direct NEDL relationship: Akhil meets monthly with the person who writes India's NEDL, and has discussed listing NeoTest "in italics" preemptively before a product exists — a first-mover advantage that could compress the listing clock significantly. (3) Direct visibility into 12–16 Carb-X–shortlisted firms via a committee seat gives real-time pipeline intelligence unavailable to external modelers. These are substantive rather than hand-wavy rebuttals. The NEDL pre-listing conversation in particular could cut years off the regulatory gate if it materialises. Akhil offered to share the firm pipeline spreadsheet and confidential meeting notes.

That said, Akhil's optimism is not baseless. His 2031 launch year maps to India's NEDL 2031 gate, which is also our central. The difference is subtle: Akhil counts commercial sales from 2031; we count meaningful public-sector uptake from 2033 (reflecting that NEDL listing does not immediately translate to procurement). This is a matter of what "launch" means in a public-sector context.

Where we genuinely disagree

Three disagreements are matters of evidence; one is a philosophical choice; one is a shared blind spot.

Genuine empirical disagreements

Disagreement 1 — Adoption rate calibration

Akhil uses the malaria RDT as his primary adoption analogue. We use TNMSC tender histories for SNCU diagnostics. Research across comparator diagnostics in 2026 found: CBNAAT/Truenat (India's best-resourced NEDL-listed molecular test) reached only ~26% test-share after ten years. CRP/PCT in East African facilities is "barely used routinely." Brazil's mandated pulse-ox screen plateaued at ~61% SUS penetration over a decade — and that had a legal mandate and donor support.

India (HB 5.5%, Leader 15%): Research confirms these are defensible central estimates. 15% Leader is "central-to-conservative"; 5.5% HB is "contingent-central" — it holds if NEDL listing triggers procurement, but falls if NEDL listing is missed.

Brazil (35%) and SSA (20–25%): Research found no documented analogue for 20–35% NICU POCT penetration without either donor saturation or legal mandate. Revised priors from comparator evidence: Brazil ~10–18%, SSA ~8–12%. The BOTEC's Brazil/SSA adoption assumptions are optimistic by a factor of ~2×. Combined CE impact of reducing to evidence-anchored values: roughly +$15–25/DALY.

Disagreement 2 — Community adherence (c2)
This is the disagreement with the largest CE effect. Akhil takes HTA referral rates at face value; we apply an independent WHO PSBI calibration that puts real-world adherence ~25% lower. The right answer here is a field question — neither of us has data from an actual NeoTest-like deployment. The first field study (ideally in a WHO PSBI-compliant setting) will settle this.
Disagreement 3 — Launch year
Akhil models 2031 India commercial launch; we model 2033 meaningful public-sector uptake. These are measuring different things. If "launch" means first commercial sale, 2031 is plausible. If "launch" means the NEDL listing that triggers public-sector procurement at scale, 2031 is aggressive and 2033 is more defensible. For CE purposes, we think the procurement-scale definition is the right one.

Philosophical choice

Cost discount rate (2% vs 9%): This is not resolvable by evidence. Akhil's 2% is standard in academic health economics for public policy analysis. Our 9% reflects the opportunity cost of philanthropic capital. For a funder benchmarking against GiveWell or other CE programs, 9% is the right rate. For a government policymaker, 2% is more defensible. The choice shifts the CE by roughly $16/DALY.

The shared blind spot

AMR DALY credit — overestimated ~10× in both models, but immaterial for the decision

Both models credit ~10% of total benefit (0.013–0.017 DALY/test) to AMR reduction from stopping unnecessary antibiotic courses. A 2026 research audit of this component found the benefit is real but the magnitude is wrong. The mechanism — fewer unnecessary antibiotic-days → lower selection pressure → fewer future resistance-driven deaths — is valid, but the near-term DALY conversion is ~7×10⁻⁴/test (0.7%) rather than 10%, because consumption-to-resistance elasticity is 0.10 and near-term BSI-DALY causation is indirect. The correct AMR credit is ~0.001 DALY/test, not 0.013.

Headline CE impact: ~$111 → ~$113/DALY. This is an intellectual-honesty correction, not a decision-changing one.

What does NOT apply here: The framing that first-line resistance "cancels" the mortality benefit is incorrect in a de-escalation design. NeoTest is used to STOP empiric antibiotics at 48–72h, not to decide whether to start them. Empiric treatment begins at hour 0 for all danger-sign infants regardless of POCT result. The POCT tells the clinician whether to continue (positive) or stop (negative) — and a positive result can prompt escalation from amp-gent to an appropriate regimen. BARNARDS-confirmed 31% amp-gent coverage makes this escalation pathway more important, not less — and makes GARDP's NeoSep1 a genuine prerequisite for the mortality mechanism to hold.

2 Community adherence: does the HTA's c2 = 0.50 hold in the field? biggest CE impact

Update from Sep 2026 call: Akhil confirmed their base was WHO PSBI's 0.38, then up-titrated for two reasons: (1) an objective diagnostic reduces referral uncertainty — families/clinicians who previously doubted whether a referral was warranted get a concrete signal, improving adherence; (2) health systems are strengthening globally, easing the infrastructure constraint. The direction is UP from 0.38, not the 0.50 we assumed independently.

This is consistent with the NeoPInS evidence (clinicians do act on clear biomarker signals) and the ProACT finding (clinicians override toward MORE treatment, not less). Whether a POCT in community or PHC settings achieves the same adherence as a biomarker in a well-resourced trial SNCU remains the empirical gap. The first deployment study in a WHO PSBI–compliant setting will be decisive.

3 AMR trajectory: have you stress-tested the community mortality mechanism against Klebsiella resistance?

Update from Sep 2026 call: Akhil offered three important clarifications: (1) their model already discounts for first-line antibiotic effectiveness generally — the diagnostic's value is not solely contingent on amp-gent working; (2) first-line regimens are not static — linezolid is increasingly used in India and has a better Klebsiella resistance profile; clinicians use facility-level antibiograms to adjust empiric choice; (3) the tertiary-hospital resistance data (96–99% amp-gent resistance for Klebsiella) is skewed by NICU outbreak patients who have been in hospital for a week or more, not the general SNCU population. District-level antibiograms likely show materially lower resistance rates.

These are substantive corrections to the "shared blind spot" framing — the resistance picture is more nuanced than the binary 96–99% figure suggests. The complementarity with GARDP's NeoSep1 (antibiotic regimen research) and their existing GARDP relationship was confirmed by Akhil. An open research question: what do community-level or district-hospital antibiograms in India and Nigeria actually show, vs the tertiary-care literature?

5 Post-AMC pricing: what does the model assume after the $30M cap is exhausted?

6 Success probability structure: does P(launch) = 0.65 interact with the marginal test calculation?

Your model applies P(launch) = 0.65 as an explicit discount to expected tests. Our model folds this into adoption rates rather than applying it as a separate multiplier. This is more than a presentational difference: if the counterfactual (no AMC) also has some positive probability of a product launching eventually, then the marginal DALY calculation should net out the counterfactual P(launch), not just the AMC-world P(launch). How does your counterfactual handle the case where a product launches anyway, just 7 years later — does the same 0.65 apply in both worlds?

7 AMR DALY credit: does 0.013 DALYs/test survive a resistance-reversal check? confirmed overestimate

Both models credit 0.013–0.017 DALYs/test for AMR reduction from stopping unnecessary antibiotic courses. A 2026 Fermi audit (antibiotic-days averted × consumption-to-resistance elasticity × resistance-BSI-DALY conversion) gives ~0.00073 DALYs/test — roughly 10–14× lower. The two inputs that drive this down: (a) consumption-to-AMR elasticity is ~0.10, not 1.0; (b) near-term resistance reversal from a single avoided course is near-zero because 68% of neonatal Klebsiella sepsis is horizontal-transmission-driven, not de novo selection from the marginal empiric course.

The stewardship benefit is real and belongs in a separate AMR-externality accounting line, but it should not be in the near-term DALY headline. Revised AMR credit: ~0.001 DALY/test (0.7% of total), not 0.013 (10%).

CE impact: ~$111 → ~$113/DALY. This is a calibration correction, not a model-invalidating finding. The mortality channel (79% of DALYs) is unaffected.

8 India clinical mode: does the CGD model expect Indian SNCUs to use NeoTest in initiation-gate or de-escalation mode? structural

NHM FBNC mandates empiric antibiotics for ALL danger-sign SNCU admissions on day 0. Real-world data (Maheshwara NICU, N=200, PMC12991650) shows 70% empiric initiation before any test result; post-rapid-dx, 50% de-escalate (vs 20% baseline). ICMR/NNF guidelines explicitly frame biomarkers as tools "to rule out" — i.e., guide stopping, not starting. This means Indian SNCUs will predominantly use NeoTest in de-escalation mode, not the initiation-gate that underpins CGD's c1 dominant result.

Our deep-dive found a convergence result: at Indian SNCU f_cf ≈ 0.80, both modes yield ~0.04 DALYs/test (initiation-gate haircut by high counterfactual collapses to ~0.041; de-escalation at Indian-calibrated parameters = mid-range ~0.04). The mode debate is second-order at SNCU level — it doesn't change the funding case. It IS first-order at community/PHC (f_cf ≈ 0.30–0.45).

Questions: (a) Does Fedorov et al. WP744 account for FBNC-mandated empiric-first practice at Indian SNCUs? (b) If de-escalation is the dominant mode, does the c1 dominant result still hold at f_cf=0.80? (c) What fraction of the c1 cohort does CGD model as truly using initiation-gate (not receiving empiric antibiotics pre-test)?

9 Community arm PSBI effective coverage: does CGD's model account for the 3.5% Nigeria implementation reality? biggest volume impact

The CGD HTA's c2 community cohort assumes functional WHO PSBI simplified-regimen infrastructure (oral amoxicillin + IM gentamicin at CHW/PHC level). Our deep-dive found effective PSBI implementation coverage is only ~3.5% in Nigeria (Dada 2020, PLOS One pone.0228718) and ~5% in Ethiopia. Nigeria represents ~72% of all global community-arm modeled tests (~294k/yr). Applying the effective coverage correction (~0.06×): Nigeria community arm falls from ~294k to ~18k tests/yr; global community arm DALYs reduced ~40–125×. This does NOT affect the hospital/SNCU arm.

Questions: (a) Does Fedorov et al. WP744 model the fraction of PHCs/CHCs with functioning PSBI regimen supply chains, or assume full PSBI coverage as a baseline? (b) If only ~3.5% of Nigeria PHCs have functional PSBI supply, does the c2 cohort TAM need a ~0.06× correction? (c) Would a parallel PSBI infrastructure investment (estimated $100–200M in Nigeria alone) be required to unlock the community arm volumes assumed in the model?

10 s_stop: what fraction of physicians actually stop antibiotics on a negative result, and does the model parameterize this? de-escalation leverage

In HIC trials (NeoPInS, Lancet 2017), protocol compliance with stopping on a negative result was 82%. In the only Indian real-world data point (Maheshwara NICU, N=200), de-escalation practice improved from 20% → 50% after rapid-dx — but didn't reach HIC levels. Open concerns — and how to resolve them

Three concerns aren't fully settled by the existing analysis. Each has a quantitative stake and a concrete path to resolution. They're open questions, not confirmed corrections — but they should inform the funding decision and the conversation with Akhil.

Concern 1 — Do Indian SNCUs actually use this test in initiation-gate mode? SETTLED — worse than expected

Why it matters: The CGD c1 dominant result (206 DALYs/1,000, dominant in 93% of PSA iterations) rests on initiation-gate deployment — the test decides whether to start antibiotics. But India's NHM FBNC protocol mandates empiric antibiotics for ALL danger-sign admissions on day 0. In practice, ~70–80% of neonates already have antibiotics before any test result is available. If Indian SNCUs instead use the test to decide whether to stop (de-escalation mode), the large mortality benefit disappears and the value becomes stewardship-only.

What we found: Maheshwara NICU (N=200, PMC12991650) — the only Indian real-world data point — shows 70% empiric initiation pre-test, rising to 50% de-escalation post-rapid-dx (vs 20% baseline). This is de-escalation, not initiation-gate. The CGD model's 89% POCT-guided fraction implies most Indian SNCUs use a "test before first dose" workflow that conflicts with FBNC mandate.

SETTLED — Sep 22 2026 deep-dive run (NeoTest_India_f_cf_day0_empirical): The convergence claim is FALSE. f_cf at Indian SNCUs is ~0.87 (band 0.80–0.92) [run], not 0.80 — 5 independent data points converge: Maheshwara NICU 95%, Ujjain NICUs 94–97%, Kodagu NICU >80%, and Heys 2025 (Neotree) showing 77–91% guideline-indicated antibiotic rate among danger-sign neonates.

The CGD's f_cf = 0.56 (used in its HTA) applies risk-stratification that does not exist in Indian district SNCUs, making the CGD's f_cf empirically wrong for the SNCU setting.

Mortality mechanism — f_cf-dependent; two settings, two outcomes

The mortality mechanism debate reduces to a single quantity: f_cf — the counterfactual antibiotic rate, meaning the fraction of at-risk neonates who would receive empiric antibiotics without the test. High f_cf (≥0.85) means doctors already treat everyone, so a test that tells them to stop is useful; a test that tells them to start is redundant or harmful. The break-even rule is Se* = f_cf (exactly, when treatment efficacy equals withholding harm). If the device operates as an initiation gate (test decides START), net benefit requires Se > f_cf; below that line it causes more harm from false-negative withholding than it saves from true-positive detection.

Community/PHC arm (f_cf ≈ 0.45–0.65) — mechanism is valid and strong. A CHW or PHC clinician sees a danger-sign neonate. Most would go untreated without the test. The break-even Se is only 0.45–0.65 — even a 65%-sensitive device is net-beneficial. This IS a treatment pathway tool. A positive result triggers first-dose antibiotics + referral; a negative result safely watches and waits. CE in this arm: ~$65–112/DALY at Se = 0.85–0.90. This is where the large mortality claim lives.

Inborn cohort arm (CGD HTA c1) — initiation-gate, dominant result. The Fedorov et al. HTA models the inborn cohort with initiation-gate: H-risk (11%) bypass → automatic treatment; L/M-risk (89%) guided by POCT. Result: 206 DALYs/1,000 PSBI infants; saves $37,342/1,000. Dominant in 93.2% of PSA iterations at $5/test. Minimum Se for net benefit = 72% — confirmed from HTA Figure 2. In practice, some Indian SNCUs may use de-escalation mode (empiric-first), which would reduce benefit to stewardship-only (~0.004–0.010 DALYs/test). This deployment mode question is the subject of active research runs.

The Se distribution risk. Deep loop analysis (NeoSep-SAA Phase 3) found P(neonatal capillary Se ≥ 0.85 under stringent prospective culture-anchored validation) ≈ 0.20. Most probability mass sits at Se 0.50–0.65 (MeMed-BV cautionary case: adult Se 89.9% → neonatal Se 37.5%). Integrating over the realistic distribution: E[DALYs/test | initiation-gate, SNCU] = −0.080 → net harm in expectation. This is why the AMC milestone must be validated on neonatal capillary blood pre-antibiotic against a culture-anchored composite reference — not transferred from adult or older-child studies.

The Se achievability question. The TPP requires ≥90% Se (essential). The HTA confirms the minimum safety floor is 72% (c1)/66% (c2) — well below TPP. A dedicated deep-dive run (NeoTest_Se90_neonatal_capillary_achievability, Sep 22 2026) now confirms P(Se≥90% on neonatal capillary blood pre-antibiotic vs CRS) ≈ 10% (range 5–18%) [run], consistent with prior estimates. P(Se≥85%, hospital TPP) ≈ 30%. P(Se≥80%, existing NeoSep-SAA territory) ≈ 55%. The decisive uncertainties are reference standard definition (~60% of total uncertainty) and multi-analyte POC translation (~30%). Recommendation from that run: gate the AMC primarily on the hospital TPP (≥85%/≥80%) and treat ≥90% as the aspirational bonus tier.

Bottom line on the 0.1098. Partially valid under the correct initiation-gate framing. The CGD HTA finds 0.167 DALYs/test (50/50 c1/c2 weighted), slightly higher than the BOTEC's 0.1392, because CGD includes additional cost savings in the benefit numerator. The BOTEC's 0.1098 DALYs/test from mortality alone is defensible but incomplete — the test also averts HAIs, AMR burden, and NEC, which add ~0.039 DALYs/test. The open questions are: (1) Is CGD's 50/50 c1/c2 weighting realistic for near-term deployment, or should it be more c1-weighted? (2) Does the dm_tx (treatment efficacy) in CGD's RR=0.35 assumption hold under BARNARDS-calibrated resistance data? These are ongoing research questions, not confirmed overstatements.

What "BCR = 31×" actually means — and why we prefer $/DALY

A benefit-cost ratio of 31× means: for every dollar of philanthropic cost, society gains $31 of welfare benefit. The "benefit" is DALYs valued at GDP per capita — roughly $2,100 for India, $4,400 for Kenya, $9,000 for Brazil, weighted by test volumes. Akhil's model gives $2.5 billion in discounted GDP/capita benefits (Summary tab B24) against $52.6M in PV costs (at 2% discount) — implying BCR of ~47× before probability adjustments, and ~31× after netting the P(launch) and P(commercialise) discounts.

The problem with using BCR for philanthropic allocation: it privileges interventions in poor countries (where GDP/capita is low, so DALYs valued at GDP/capita are cheap relative to cost) over rich-country interventions, which is not the right criterion. It also makes the number incomparable to GiveWell's benchmark, which uses a standard cost-per-DALY metric that doesn't depend on the GDP of the recipient country.

CE ($/DALY) solves this by asking the neutral question: how many dollars of philanthropic cost per unit of health benefit? At our revised ~$110–170/DALY range, NeoTest sits near the GiveWell top-charity bar (~$50–100) — potentially within range depending on adoption speed. The original models ($111–$119) and our prior intermediate estimate ($1,218) are both superseded; the current range reflects retracting two errors from our analysis after Akhil's rebuttal.
