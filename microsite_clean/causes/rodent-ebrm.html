Safer Landscapes — Rodent Fund

EBRM in India

Ecologically-Based Rodent Management as an agricultural income intervention for India's smallholder farmers. $5M pilot + $1.5M RFC R&D, milestone-gated.

Verdict

Fund $5M EBRM pilot as an evidence and capacity bet, not a DALY buy. At operational scale in high-damage rice areas, EBRM could reach $150–240/DALY-eq — approaching GiveWell bar. But zero India-specific field trials exist. The $5M buys India-specific evidence in a $5B/yr neglected problem. If the pilot succeeds, the catalytic value (enabling government scale-up) yields ~$300–500/DALY-eq. If it fails, you've spent $5M to learn that — a defensible VoI investment.

Fund $1.5M RFC R&D as a small option-value bet, strictly milestone-gated. The science does not support fertility control for open-field Indian agriculture — the immigration problem is fatal. Do not commit $50–100M to RFC under any scenario.

The problem

India loses an estimated 5–10% of cereal crops annually to rodent damage — approximately $5 billion per year. In NE India during bamboo-flowering mast events, losses reach 14–48% of harvest. The burden falls almost entirely on smallholder farmers (average holding <1 ha) with near-zero income resilience.

Current management is chemically-dependent, ecologically damaging, and mostly ineffective at landscape scale. Outside Andhra Pradesh's state model, coordinated non-chemical rodent management is essentially absent.

What EBRM is

Ecologically-Based Rodent Management is a suite of non-chemical techniques that disrupt rodent food availability and reproduction cycles rather than targeting individuals with poison.

Core components

  • Trap Barrier Systems (TBS): A strip of crop planted 2–3 weeks early attracts rodents into a fenced trap zone. One TBS unit protects ~10 ha. (IRRI Knowledge Bank)
  • Community-synchronized planting: Narrow planting windows mismatch rodent reproduction with food availability, suppressing between-season population growth.
  • Habitat management: Bund maintenance, vegetation clearing, irrigation timing to expose burrows, promoting natural predators (barn owls, snakes).
  • Village coordination committees: Community-level synchronization — the critical coordination layer that ties all components together.

Evidence base

Strong international evidence shows EBRM producing 6–32% yield increases in lowland irrigated rice, with benefit-cost ratios of 3:1 to 11:1. (Stuart 2019, Cambodia; Singleton 2004, Indonesia)

Critical caveat
All strong evidence comes from SE Asian lowland irrigated rice. There are zero published EBRM field trials on Indian crops. Effectiveness on wheat, maize, millet, and sugarcane is entirely unvalidated. MetaMeta and PRADAN have tested EBRM in 4 villages in Madhya Pradesh (Down to Earth 2023) — that is the totality of India evidence.

BOTEC chain (central case)

Income gains from reduced crop losses are converted to welfare using GiveWell's framework: income gain → Doubled Consumption Years (DCY) → DALY-equivalent (2.5 DCY = 1 DALY-eq). Health co-benefits from rodent-borne disease reduction are not included — no evidence links EBRM to disease reduction.

RFC: why not

Rodent Fertility Control aims to render female rodents infertile via oral bait, reducing populations over time. Only two products are registered anywhere globally: ContraPest (US) and EP-1 (China/Tanzania). The definitive review (Jacoblinnert et al. 2022) concludes: "Effective fertility control of rodents has not been demonstrated at landscape scales."

At $5M R&D reaching 100K households conditionally: expected $/DALY-eq = $860,000 — ~10,000× the GiveWell bar. Additionally, SenesTech (sole manufacturer of ContraPest) has a $3.9M market cap and $6.4M net loss in FY2025. India's key pest species (Bandicota bengalensis, Rattus rattus) have never been tested with any fertility control agent.

Do not fund at scale
RFC should receive at most $1.5M in milestone-gated R&D to test lab efficacy on Indian species. Any commitment beyond this is not defensible on current evidence. Kill T1 if <80% lab litter reduction is achieved.

Key uncertainties

Does EBRM work in India at all?

The $5M pilot IS the experiment. Every EBRM field trial with yield data comes from SE Asian lowland irrigated rice. India's species mix, cropping systems, and smallholder landscape differ. The MetaMeta/PRADAN 4-village test in MP is promising but unpublished and small.

Does EBRM work on non-rice crops?

All strong evidence is rice-only. TBS has theoretical generalizability to wheat, maize, and millet — untested. The pilot should deliberately include wheat/maize areas in MP alongside rice areas in Jharkhand to test crop generalizability. This is a major design requirement.

Will community coordination hold?

EBRM requires synchronized action across a village — if farmers defect on synchronized planting, the coordination logic collapses. India's fragmented smallholder landscape (avg <1 ha) may make coordination harder than in SE Asia, where community agriculture institutions are stronger. PRADAN's SHG network is the key hypothesis: if SHGs can sustain EBRM coordination, this becomes tractable.

Will benefits persist without support?

Persistence is the single most important parameter in the BOTEC (5.2× swing factor). No data exists on post-program EBRM persistence in any setting. M&E design should explicitly track post-support behavior at 12 and 24 months after handoff.

Will state governments adopt and co-finance?

The Andhra Pradesh model works because the state co-finances and extends through the agriculture department. Without this, philanthropic funding alone cannot reach meaningful scale — PRADAN covers 7,434 villages, but there are ~600,000 villages in rural India. The MoU attempt with Bihar or Jharkhand is a critical early gate (M6 kill-switch).

What a first grant teaches
The $5M pilot directly answers: (1) Does EBRM work in India? (2) Does it work on non-rice crops? (3) Can PRADAN's SHG network coordinate it? (4) What does $/HH actually cost at India wages? (5) Is there a government scale-up pathway? A negative answer on any of these is a legitimate and valuable research outcome — it prevents much larger misdirected capital.
